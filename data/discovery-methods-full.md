## Architecture Note – Read Before Assessing

### What Discovery Karaoke is

**Discovery Karaoke** is a Claude Code skill and companion web app that helps product teams select the right discovery method for their situation. The name is the thesis: most teams perform discovery rituals – running interviews, usability tests, or sprints – without connecting them to the risk they are trying to reduce or the evidence they actually need. That is karaoke: technically doing the thing, but not for real.

The framework answers one practical question: *given my risk, my stage, and my constraints – what method should I run, and what evidence will it give me?* The decision logic is: **Risk → Stage → Method → Evidence**.

**Target users**: product managers, product designers, and cross-functional product teams in 2026 who need to make better discovery decisions under real constraints – time, budget, team size, product maturity, regulatory exposure.

**Framework home**: The Thinking Lens (the-thinking-lens.com). Author: Amodiovalerio Verde.

---

### What this database is – and is not

**This is a practitioner decision support tool, not an academic survey.** The scope is deliberately bounded:

- **In scope**: Methods a product team can actually run to reduce build risk – generative research, evaluative testing, experimentation, analytics, compliance assessment, and the framing tools that direct these activities
- **Out of scope**: Academic research methodology for its own sake, delivery/planning frameworks (story mapping, roadmapping), pure business strategy tools (BMC, Lean Canvas), and facilitation templates that do not generate evidence
- **Not an encyclopedia**: The goal is not to catalogue every known research or strategy method. Methods are included because they are commonly needed and commonly misused – the "When NOT to Use" sections are as important as the "How to Apply" sections
- **Not a UX research library**: Compliance and feasibility risk are first-class. 10 compliance methods (M75–M84) and 3 AI feasibility methods (M89–M91) are included because modern product work requires them, not as a concession to comprehensiveness

**Scope curation decisions that have been made:**
- Deprecated 12 methods (M34, M40, M41, M43, M51, M52, M61, M62, M63, M85, M86, M87) as out-of-scope for discovery – they belong to delivery planning, synthesis, or business design
- Retained 13 framing/synthesis/practice tools (Context Tools) in the DB with explicit notes – they appear to prevent misuse, not because they generate evidence
- Superseded 3 methods (M25, M30, M46) rather than deleting – preserves searchability and institutional memory

**The anti-karaoke design principle** runs through every entry: methods are organised by the risk they address, not by category popularity. Evidence strength is explicitly rated. Non-evidence-generating tools are clearly labelled. Every method has a "When NOT to Use" section. These are not academic completeness features – they are misuse prevention.

### Technical architecture

This file is the **editorial master layer**. It is intentionally optimised for human readability. Do not assess it as a raw machine-readable schema.

**What this file is:**
- The canonical source of truth for all method content (descriptions, origins, sources, guidance, metadata)
- Written in structured Markdown with consistent field patterns – every method follows the same field sequence
- Designed to be read, edited, and reviewed by practitioners and maintainers without tooling

**What this file is not:**
- The application data layer. That is `data.js`, generated by `parse_data.py`
- A flat JSON schema. Comma-separated fields (Risk Addressed, Discovery Stage, Team Size) become typed arrays in the derived JSON
- The web app filter model. The parser derives `primary_risk` (first value in Risk Addressed) and `primary_stage` (first value in Discovery Stage) for ranking – these are not explicit fields here

**Derived assets (do not edit directly – regenerate from this file):**
- `data.js` – parsed JSON for the web app (vverde.github.io/discovery-karaoke-engine/)
- `SKILL.md` – Claude Code skill, filtered to Active methods with practitioner guidance

**Generation pipeline:**
```
discovery-methods-full.md  →  parse_data.py  →  data.js  →  web app
                           →  SKILL.md (manual sync until scripted)
```

**Parser rules applied on generation (not explicit in this file):**
- Comma-separated fields → arrays: risks, stages, team_size, related_methods, prerequisites, alternatives
- `primary_risk` derived from first value in Risk Addressed
- `primary_stage` derived from first value in Discovery Stage
- Superseded entries filtered from active set
- `Needs Existing Users / Needs Live Traffic / Needs Legal Review` → boolean flags

**Known design tensions (intentional, not bugs):**
- 83 entries span Methods, Frameworks, Synthesis Tools, and two Operating Practices – mixed types in one flat list, by design. Category + Type fields carry the distinction.
- Evidence Strength: N/A marks all non-evidence-generating entries (13 context tools). These tools appear in the DB to prevent misuse, not because they generate evidence.
- Best For Company is low-signal (43/80 = Any). It is metadata, not a primary filter. The three constraint fields (Needs Existing Users, Needs Live Traffic, Needs Legal Review) are the operative filters.
- ID sequence is non-contiguous (gaps at M34/M40/M41/M43/M50–M52/M61–M63/M85–M87) – result of scope curation across sessions. IDs are stable identifiers, not sequence numbers. M25/M30/M46 are present but Superseded.
- Portfolio skews toward Validate and Solution Space by design – that is where karaoke is most common and most damaging. Explore and Problem Space coverage is sufficient for practitioner use but not encyclopedic.

- **Complexity**: Simple | Simple-Moderate | Moderate | Moderate-Complex | Complex
- **Team Size**: Solo | Small (2-5) | Cross-functional (5+) (multi-value, comma-separated)

- **Needs Existing Users**: Yes | No

- **Needs Live Traffic**: Yes | No
  - *Yes*: requires active users currently using a live instrumented product
- **Needs Legal Review**: Yes | No
  - *Yes*: requires legal, compliance, or DPO expertise to execute, or has formal regulatory standing

## Field Rubrics

### Effort

| Level | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| Low | Solo, minimal setup, no recruiting; can start immediately |
| Low-Medium | Light setup or coordination; some analysis but no participant management overhead |
| Medium | Meaningful planning, recruiting, or synthesis; manageable by one practitioner or small team |
| Medium-High | Multiple phases with participant management, longitudinal overhead, or cross-functional coordination |
| High | Heavy fieldwork, immersion, specialist execution, or large synthesis burden |

### Complexity

| Level | Definition |
|-------|-----------|
| Simple | Low interpretation risk; easy to run correctly |
| Simple-Moderate | Some craft required but repeatable by a solid generalist |
| Moderate | Requires research skill in design, moderation, or synthesis |
| Moderate-Complex | Several dependencies or significant interpretation risk |
| Complex | Specialist judgment required; high misuse risk; hard to run well without training |

### Cost Range

| Level | Definition | Indicative range |
|-------|-----------|-----------------|
| Time-Only | Internal team time only; no external spend of any kind | $0 external |
| Low | Light external spend – modest incentives, basic tooling subscription, or minor logistics; no dedicated budget line needed in most teams | ~$100–2,000 per study |
| Medium | Dedicated budget required – meaningful participant compensation, specialist tooling, external facilitation, or field logistics | ~$2,000–20,000 |
| High | Major investment – extended fieldwork, specialist contractors, multi-market travel, large participant panels, or significant third-party services | ~$20,000–100,000 |
| Very High | Exceptional resource commitment – regulatory-grade audits, large-scale fielding, or multi-wave specialist programmes | $100,000+ |

> Ranges are directional anchors for a US/Western European market context. They vary significantly by region, team size, recruitment channel, and execution approach. Use them to order-of-magnitude budget estimates and distinguish levels – not as quotes.

**Cost Notes format**: list primary cost drivers in plain language only. Do not include currency figures, ranges, or totals – those belong in the rubric above, not in individual method entries.

### Time Notes format

List the phases that drive duration: `[phase 1] + [phase 2] + [phase 3]`. For Ongoing methods, note the setup phase and recurring cadence separately. Do not repeat the Time to Execute range.

## METHOD 1: User Interviews (Problem Discovery)

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: One-on-one conversations with users or potential users to understand their problems, needs, behaviors, and motivations in their own words. The foundational generative research method.

**Origin**: Rooted in ethnographic and clinical interviewing traditions. Popularized in product context by Steve Portigal (*Interviewing Users*, 2013) and Rob Fitzpatrick (*The Mom Test*, 2013).
**Authoritative Source**: Portigal, S. (2013). *Interviewing Users: How to Uncover Compelling Insights*. Rosenfeld Media. Also: Fitzpatrick, R. (2013). *The Mom Test*. CreateSpace.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: recruiting + interviews + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: participant recruiting + incentives + transcription/support tooling

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define research questions** – Write 3-5 specific questions you want to answer. Focus on behaviors and needs, not feature requests. (2-3 hours)
2. **Create a discussion guide** – 10-15 open-ended questions organized by theme. Start broad ("Tell me about the last time you…"), then narrow. Avoid leading questions. (3-4 hours)
3. **Recruit participants** – Screen for your target segment. Aim for 5-8 interviews as a starting point (typically diminishing returns after 8-12, though saturation depends on segment diversity, scope, and question complexity). Use screener surveys. (1-2 weeks)
4. **Conduct interviews** – 30-60 minutes each. Record with consent. Use active listening. Follow the energy—go deep when you hear something unexpected. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Synthesize findings** – Transcribe key quotes. Affinity map themes. Look for patterns across 3+ participants. Document surprises. (1-2 days)
6. **Share insights** – Create a findings deck with themes, quotes, and implications. Tie each finding to a product decision. (half day)

**When to Use**:
- You don't understand the problem space well enough to form hypotheses
- You're entering a new market or user segment
- Quantitative data shows WHAT is happening but not WHY

**When NOT to Use**:
- You already have clear hypotheses and need to test them (→ use experiments instead)
- The question is quantitative ("how many?" "what percentage?") (→ use surveys or analytics)
- You're using interviews to validate what you already believe (confirmation bias trap)

**Output/Deliverable**: Research findings report with themes, user quotes, behavioral patterns, and opportunity areas. Often includes an affinity diagram.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: rich qualitative insights but small sample, not statistically generalizable
**AI Augmentation**: AI can draft discussion guides, transcribe recordings, and cluster themes from transcripts.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 2: Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD) Interviews

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Structured interviews focused on the "job" a user is trying to accomplish, exploring the circumstances, motivations, and desired outcomes that drive switching behavior. Goes beyond surface needs to underlying motivations.

**Origin**: Clayton Christensen (Harvard Business School), further developed by Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek at The Re-Wired Group. Switch Interview technique from Moesta & Spiek.
**Authoritative Source**: Christensen, C. et al. (2016). *Competing Against Luck*. Harper Business. Also: Kalbach, J. (2020). *The Jobs To Be Done Playbook*. Rosenfeld Media. Moesta, B. (2020). *Demand-Side Sales 101*.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: especially powerful for Scaleup and Enterprise navigating market expansion
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: recruiting recent switchers + switch interviews + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: specialized recruiting for recent switchers + trained interviewer time + participant incentives

**How to Apply**:
1. **Identify the switching moment** – Find users who recently adopted, switched to, or abandoned a product/solution. These are your interview candidates. (1 week recruiting)
2. **Conduct the Switch Interview** – Use the Forces of Progress framework: Push (frustration with current), Pull (attraction of new), Anxiety (fear of change), Habit (comfort with status quo). Timeline the purchase/adoption story in detail. (60-90 min per interview)
3. **Map the job story** – "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]." Identify functional, emotional, and social dimensions of the job. (During synthesis)
4. **Cluster jobs** – Group interviews by common jobs. Look for jobs that are underserved (high importance, low satisfaction). (1-2 days)
5. **Prioritize jobs** – Rank jobs by prevalence across interviews and severity of the push/pull forces. Jobs that appear frequently with strong push (frustration with current solution) and strong pull (attraction toward yours) are highest-opportunity targets. (1 day)
6. **Translate to product strategy** – Map high-opportunity jobs to potential features, positioning, or new products. (Workshop, half day)

**When to Use**:
- You need to understand WHY users switch (or don't switch) to your product
- You're defining or repositioning a product category
- Feature-based research isn't revealing meaningful insights

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need quick tactical input on UI/UX details (→ use usability testing)
- The product category is well-established and you're optimizing, not innovating
- You don't have access to recent switchers (the technique requires people who recently made a change)

**Output/Deliverable**: Job stories, Forces of Progress diagrams, job opportunity ranking, and strategic recommendations for product/market fit.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: deep causal understanding of behavior, but requires skilled interviewing
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help analyze interview transcripts for Forces of Progress patterns and draft initial job stories for researcher validation.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 3: Contextual Inquiry

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Observing and interviewing users in their actual work or life context (home, office, field) while they perform real tasks. Reveals tacit knowledge, workarounds, and environmental factors that users can't or won't articulate in a lab setting.

**Origin**: Hugh Beyer and Karen Holtzblatt, developed at Digital Equipment Corporation in the 1980s.
**Authoritative Source**: Beyer, H. & Holtzblatt, K. (1997). *Contextual Design: Defining Customer-Centered Systems*. Morgan Kaufmann. Updated: Holtzblatt, K. & Beyer, H. (2016). *Contextual Design: Design for Life*, 2nd Edition.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: typically researcher + note-taker
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: justifies investment for complex B2B or domain-specific products
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: planning + field sessions + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: site access coordination + travel or remote observation logistics + participant compensation + synthesis effort

**How to Apply**:
1. **Plan the inquiry** – Define what activities/tasks to observe. Identify 6-12 participants in their real environment. Schedule 2-3 hour sessions. (1 week)
2. **Establish the master/apprentice model** – Position yourself as apprentice learning from the user (the master). They lead, you follow and ask questions. (At session start)
3. **Observe and interview simultaneously** – Watch the user work. When you see something interesting, pause and ask "What just happened? Why did you do that? What were you thinking?" (2-3 hours per session)
4. **Document artifacts** – Photograph the environment, tools, sticky notes, whiteboards, workarounds. These reveal unarticulated needs. (During session)
5. **Build interpretation notes** – After each session, write up key observations, surprises, breakdowns, and workarounds within 24 hours. (1-2 hours per session)
6. **Create work models** – Synthesize across sessions into flow models, sequence models, artifact models, cultural models, and physical models. (2-5 days)

**When to Use**:
- Users operate in complex, specialized environments (hospitals, warehouses, factories, trading floors)
- You suspect users have workarounds or tacit knowledge they can't articulate
- Previous interview-only research failed to reveal actionable insights

**When NOT to Use**:
- The activity is purely digital and synchronous remote observation is impractical (→ session recordings may suffice; note that remote contextual inquiry—screen share + think-aloud—is a valid adaptation for digital workflows)
- You have time or budget constraints that prevent field visits (→ remote diary studies as alternative)
- Privacy or security restrictions prevent on-site observation (→ consider simulated environments)

**Output/Deliverable**: Work models (flow, sequence, artifact, cultural, physical), affinity wall, design insights, and personas grounded in observed behavior.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: direct observation of real behavior in context
**AI Augmentation**: AI can transcribe field recordings and help organize interpretation notes, but cannot replace the in-person observation itself.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 4: Ethnographic Research

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Deep immersive research where researchers spend extended time in the user's environment, culture, or community to understand behaviors, values, and social dynamics. Goes beyond task observation to cultural understanding.

**Origin**: Rooted in cultural anthropology (Bronisław Malinowski, 1920s). Applied to design by IDEO, Doblin Group, and Jan Chipchase.
**Authoritative Source**: Chipchase, J. (2013). *Hidden in Plain Sight*. Harper Business. Also: Salvador, T. et al. (1999). "Design Ethnography." *Design Management Journal*. IDEO's Human-Centered Design Toolkit.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: often specialized researchers
**Best For Company**: Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: requires significant investment; strong ROI potential for products serving unfamiliar markets/cultures
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 4-12 weeks
**Time Order**: Months
**Time Notes**: access-building + immersion + synthesis
**Cost Range**: High
**Cost Notes**: extended researcher immersion + travel + access-building + deep synthesis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the cultural question** – What do you need to understand about people's lives, values, or practices that standard research can't reveal? (1-2 days planning)
2. **Gain access and build trust** – Enter the community or environment. Spend time without a specific agenda at first. Build rapport. (1-2 weeks)
3. **Immerse and observe** – Participate in daily activities. Use multiple data collection methods: observation, conversation, photography, artifacts. Look for rituals, unspoken rules, and tensions. (2-8 weeks)
4. **Maintain a field journal** – Record daily observations, reflections, and emerging patterns. Separate observations from interpretations. (Ongoing)
5. **Triangulate data** – Cross-reference observations with interviews, artifacts, and secondary sources. Look for converging evidence. (During and after immersion)
6. **Synthesize into cultural insights** – Create narratives, cultural frameworks, and design implications. Present as stories, not just findings. (2-4 weeks)

**When to Use**:
- You're designing for an unfamiliar culture, community, or context
- Previous research methods have failed to explain user behavior
- You need to understand the emotional and social dimensions of product use

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need quick answers for an upcoming sprint (→ far too slow)
- The target users are easily accessible and well-understood
- Budget and timeline don't allow for immersive research (→ use contextual inquiry as a lighter alternative)

**Output/Deliverable**: Cultural insights report, ethnographic narratives, personas, design principles, and strategic frameworks grounded in deep cultural understanding.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: deepest possible understanding of human context
**AI Augmentation**: AI can assist with field note analysis and pattern identification across large volumes of ethnographic data, but cannot conduct immersive observation.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 5: Diary Studies

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Participants self-report their experiences, behaviors, and emotions over an extended period (days to weeks) in their natural context. Captures longitudinal data about real-world behavior that single-session research misses.

**Origin**: Rooted in psychology's Experience Sampling Method (Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 1987), a signal-contingent or time-based sampling approach. UX diary studies are a broader family: participants report at natural trigger moments or defined intervals, with more flexible format and cadence than strict ESM. Applied to UX by researchers at Microsoft, Google, and academic HCI labs.
**Authoritative Source**: Bolger, N. et al. (2003). "Diary Methods." *Annual Review of Psychology*. Also: Flaherty, K. (2016). "Diary Studies: Understanding Long-Term User Behavior." Nielsen Norman Group.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: especially useful for products with habitual or longitudinal usage patterns
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: protocol design + onboarding + diary period + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: participant incentives for sustained engagement + diary tooling/workflow + follow-up interviews

**How to Apply**:
1. **Design the diary protocol** – Define what participants should report, when, and how. Create structured prompts (e.g., "Each time you [trigger], tell us about it"). Choose the medium: app, SMS, email, physical journal. (2-3 days)
2. **Recruit and onboard participants** – Select 10-15 participants. Run an onboarding session explaining the protocol. Do a practice entry together. (1 week)
3. **Manage the study period** – Send reminders. Monitor participation rates. Follow up with low-responders. Send encouragement. (1-4 weeks)
4. **Conduct follow-up interviews** – After the diary period, interview participants to debrief. Walk through their entries, clarify, and probe deeper on interesting moments. (30-60 min each)
5. **Analyze longitudinal patterns** – Look for patterns over time: frequency, triggers, emotional arcs, behavior changes, abandoned habits. (2-3 days)
6. **Map the experience timeline** – Create a longitudinal experience map showing how needs, behaviors, and satisfaction change over the study period. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- Behavior changes over time (onboarding, habit formation, seasonal patterns)
- You need to capture in-the-moment experiences that users forget in retrospective interviews
- You're studying infrequent but important events (e.g., filing taxes, booking travel)

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need results in less than 2 weeks (→ too slow)
- Participant burden is too high for your audience (executives, patients)
- The behavior you're studying happens in a single session (→ usability testing is faster)

**Output/Deliverable**: Longitudinal experience maps, behavior pattern analysis, trigger-behavior-outcome chains, and rich contextual data about real-world product use.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: real-world longitudinal data, but relies on self-reporting accuracy
**AI Augmentation**: AI can analyze diary entries at scale, detect sentiment trends over time, and flag notable patterns for researcher follow-up.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 6: Support Ticket Analysis

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Systematic analysis of customer support tickets, help desk logs, and complaint records to identify recurring problems, pain points, and unmet needs directly from user-initiated feedback.

**Origin**: Voice of the Customer (VoC) and service analytics practices. Used in product discovery as a secondary signal source alongside interviews and other generative methods.
**Authoritative Source**: Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits*. Product Talk. Goodman, E., Kuniavsky, M. & Moed, A. (2012). *Observing the User Experience*. Morgan Kaufmann (Chapter on analyzing existing data sources).

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Optimize
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: requires existing product with support channel
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-5 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: data export + tagging + frequency analysis + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal analysis time + data access + tagging effort

**How to Apply**:
1. **Export ticket data** – Pull 3-6 months of support tickets from your help desk system. Include categories, tags, and full descriptions. (1-2 hours)
2. **Categorize and tag** – Create a taxonomy of issue types. Code each ticket by problem category, severity, and user segment. Use existing tags as starting point. (1-2 days)
3. **Quantify frequency and impact** – Count tickets per category. Cross-reference with user segments, plan types, or lifecycle stages. Identify the top 10 issues by volume. (Half day)
4. **Identify root causes** – For the top issues, read individual tickets to understand the underlying problem. Are they usability issues, missing features, bugs, or misaligned expectations? (1 day)
5. **Map to product opportunities** – Connect support themes to product backlog items or new opportunities. Estimate impact of fixing each issue. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- You have an existing product with a support channel and want to identify pain points
- You need to prioritize bug fixes or UX improvements based on actual user impact
- You want to complement other research with naturally occurring user feedback

**When NOT to Use**:
- You don't have a product yet (no tickets to analyze)
- Your support data is too noisy or poorly categorized to be useful
- You're looking for new opportunities (support tickets reflect known problems, not unmet needs)
- You're trying to understand root causes without follow-up (tickets show symptoms, not underlying motivations—pair with interviews to go deeper)

**Output/Deliverable**: Categorized issue taxonomy, frequency analysis, top-10 pain points ranked by impact, and product opportunity recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: real user-initiated feedback, but biased toward vocal users and acute problems—silent churners are invisible
**AI Augmentation**: AI excels here—can categorize tickets, extract themes, detect sentiment, and identify emerging trends across thousands of tickets in minutes.

## METHOD 10: Focus Groups

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Facilitated group discussion (6-10 participants) exploring attitudes, perceptions, and reactions to concepts, products, or ideas. Leverages group dynamics to surface diverse perspectives and social influences.

**Origin**: Robert K. Merton's "focused interview" work in the 1940s is widely cited as a precursor. Modern focus group practice as a market research tool developed through the 1950s–70s and is related to but not identical with Merton's original method.
**Authoritative Source**: Krueger, R.A. & Casey, M.A. (2015). *Focus Groups: A Practical Guide for Applied Research*, 5th Ed. SAGE. Also: Morgan, D.L. (1997). *Focus Groups as Qualitative Research*. SAGE.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: facilitator + note-taker + observers
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: common in Enterprise/consumer products with research budgets
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: recruiting + sessions + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: recruiting + facilitation + participant incentives + facility or platform + synthesis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define objectives** – What specific questions will the focus group answer? Focus groups are best for exploring attitudes, perceptions, and social dynamics. (1 day)
2. **Design the moderator guide** – Create a 90-minute structured discussion guide with warm-up, core topics, activities (card sorting, concept reactions), and wrap-up. (1-2 days)
3. **Recruit participants** – 6-10 per group, run 2-4 groups for pattern reliability. Segment groups by user type. Avoid mixing customers with non-customers. (1-2 weeks)
4. **Facilitate the session** – Manage group dynamics. Ensure quieter participants speak. Use projective techniques for sensitive topics. Record with consent. (90 min per group)
5. **Analyze group dynamics** – Note where the group agreed/disagreed, who influenced whom, and what topics generated energy. Individual opinions ≠ group consensus. (1-2 days)
6. **Report findings** – Summarize themes, notable quotes, areas of consensus/divergence, and implications for product decisions. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- You want to explore attitudes and perceptions in a social context
- You need to understand how people influence each other's opinions about a category
- You want to quickly explore reactions to multiple concepts or ideas

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to understand individual behavior (focus groups reveal attitudes, not actions)
- Sensitive or private topics where group pressure inhibits honesty (→ 1:1 interviews)
- You're treating focus group preferences as purchase intent (classic mistake—"liking" in a group ≠ buying)

**Output/Deliverable**: Thematic analysis with group consensus/divergence map, concept reaction summaries, and recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Weak-Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: rich attitudinal data but subject to groupthink, social desirability, and dominance bias
**AI Augmentation**: AI can transcribe and analyze multi-speaker recordings, identify dominant vs. quiet participants, and extract themes across multiple sessions.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 11: Surveys (Problem Validation)

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Structured questionnaires distributed to a large sample to quantify the prevalence, severity, and segmentation of problems identified through qualitative research. Measures how widespread a problem is.

**Origin**: Social science survey methodology. Applied to product by Sean Ellis ("How disappointed would you be?" question) and Teresa Torres.
**Authoritative Source**: Dillman, D.A. et al. (2014). *Internet, Phone, Mail, and Mixed-Mode Surveys*, 4th Ed. Wiley. Also: Ellis, S. (2017). *Hacking Growth*. Crown Business. Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: design + distribution + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: survey tooling + distribution + panel or recruitment effort + analysis time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Ground in qualitative insights** – NEVER write a survey before qualitative research. Use interview/observation findings to write informed questions. (Prerequisite)
2. **Write clear questions** – 10-20 questions max. Use Likert scales for severity, multiple choice for frequency, and ONE open-ended question max. Avoid double-barreled and leading questions. (1-2 days)
3. **Pilot test** – Send to 5-10 people first. Check for confusion, time to complete (aim <7 minutes), and data quality. (1-2 days)
4. **Distribute to target sample** – Aim for 100-500+ responses for statistical power. Use in-product intercepts, email, social, or panel services. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Analyze quantitatively** – Calculate frequencies, cross-tabulations, and segments. Look for patterns by user segment, problem frequency, and severity scores. (1-2 days)
6. **Triangulate with qualitative data** – Compare survey results with interview findings. Do the numbers match the stories? (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- You've done qualitative research and want to quantify how widespread a problem is
- You need to segment users by problem severity or behavior
- You want statistical evidence to support a product decision

**When NOT to Use**:
- You haven't done qualitative research yet (you'll write the wrong questions)
- The question requires nuance that closed-ended questions can't capture (→ interviews)
- You're surveying to avoid talking to users (the Survey Spray anti-pattern)

**Output/Deliverable**: Quantified problem severity, user segmentation, statistical validation of qualitative findings, and prioritized problem list.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: large sample, quantifiable, but limited by question design and response bias
**AI Augmentation**: AI can analyze open-ended responses, segment data, identify patterns, and generate cross-tabulations.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M1 or equivalent qualitative discovery input

## METHOD 12: Surveys (Satisfaction – NPS/CSAT/CES)

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Ongoing measurement of customer satisfaction (CSAT), loyalty (NPS – Net Promoter Score), and effort (CES – Customer Effort Score) to track product health, identify at-risk segments, and measure the impact of changes over time. Each metric suits different contexts; evidence does not support a universal best choice across industries or product types.

**Origin**: NPS created by Fred Reichheld and Bain & Company (2003). CSAT rooted in customer satisfaction research (1970s-80s).
**Authoritative Source**: Reichheld, F. (2003). "The One Number You Need to Grow." *Harvard Business Review*. Also: Reichheld, F. & Markey, R. (2011). *The Ultimate Question 2.0*. Harvard Business Review Press. For CES: Dixon, M. et al. (2010). "Stop Trying to Delight Your Customers." *Harvard Business Review*. For CSAT: Peterson, R. & Wilson, W. (1992). "Measuring Customer Satisfaction." *Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science*.

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Viability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: requires existing product with users
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: Ongoing
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: setup + recurring collection + trend analysis + follow-up
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: survey tooling + recurring analysis + response follow-up effort

**How to Apply**:
1. **Choose your metric** – NPS ("How likely are you to recommend…" 0-10) for loyalty/advocacy. CSAT ("How satisfied are you with…" 1-5) for specific interaction satisfaction. CES (Customer Effort Score) for ease of use. (1 hour)
2. **Design the survey** – Keep it to 2-3 questions: the score question + "What's the primary reason for your score?" (open-ended) + optional segmentation question. (1-2 hours)
3. **Set trigger and frequency** – Decide when to ask: post-interaction (CSAT), periodic (NPS quarterly), or event-based (after onboarding). Avoid survey fatigue. (1 hour)
4. **Deploy and collect** – Use in-product modals, email, or SMS. Target 20-30% response rate. Ensure sample is representative. (Ongoing)
5. **Analyze trends** – Track scores over time. Segment by cohort, plan type, and usage level. Read every open-ended response (or use AI). (Weekly/monthly)
6. **Close the feedback loop** – Follow up with detractors. Share promoter quotes. Use trends to prioritize product work. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- You need an ongoing pulse on customer satisfaction
- You want to measure the impact of product changes over time
- You need to identify at-risk customers for churn prevention

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're using NPS as your sole discovery method (it tells you satisfaction, not what to build)
- Your product is too new to have meaningful longitudinal data
- You're treating NPS as a goal rather than a signal (Goodhart's Law risk)

**Output/Deliverable**: NPS/CSAT scores over time, segment analysis, open-ended feedback themes, and churn risk indicators.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: useful as a health metric, but NPS alone doesn't explain WHY or WHAT to do
**AI Augmentation**: AI excels at analyzing open-ended NPS/CSAT responses at scale—theme extraction, sentiment analysis, and trend detection.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 13: Card Sorting

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Participants organize topics, labels, or content items into groups that make sense to them, revealing their mental models for information architecture. Open sorts let users create categories; closed sorts test existing categories.

**Origin**: Related to sorting and construct-elicitation traditions in psychology (Kelly, 1955, and others). Applied to information architecture as a product practice by Donna Spencer and the Nielsen Norman Group.
**Authoritative Source**: Spencer, D. (2009). *Card Sorting: Designing Usable Categories*. Rosenfeld Media. Also: Spencer, D. (2004). "Card Sorting: A Definitive Guide." Boxes and Arrows.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: setup + sessions + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: card sorting tooling + participant recruitment or incentives + analysis time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Prepare content items** – Write 30-60 items (features, pages, topics) on cards. Each item should be a noun or short phrase. Use real content labels. (2-4 hours)
2. **Choose sort type** – Open sort: users create and name their own groups (for exploration). Closed sort: you provide the categories, users place items (for validation). Hybrid: users can modify provided categories. (1 hour)
3. **Run sessions** – In-person (5-8 participants, observe and discuss) or remote (15-30+ participants using a card sorting tool). (1-5 days)
4. **Analyze results** – Create a similarity matrix showing how often items were grouped together. Use dendrogram analysis for open sorts. (1-2 days)
5. **Derive information architecture** – Translate clusters into navigation structure, category labels, and content hierarchy. Test with tree testing. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- You're designing or redesigning navigation/information architecture
- You want to understand how users think about your content/feature categories
- You need to validate category labels and groupings

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're testing task flows or interactions (→ usability testing)
- Your IA is simple enough that best practices suffice (e.g., settings page)
- You have fewer than 15 items to sort (too few to reveal patterns)

**Output/Deliverable**: Similarity matrix, dendrograms, recommended category structure, and label recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: reveals mental models effectively, but doesn't test task completion
**AI Augmentation**: AI can analyze card sort data, generate similarity matrices, and suggest optimal groupings based on co-occurrence patterns.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 14: Tree Testing

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Participants are given a text-only version of a site's hierarchy (no design) and asked to find specific items, testing whether the information architecture is intuitive without visual design influence.

**Origin**: Developed by Dave O'Brien at Optimal Workshop (mid-2000s) as a complement to card sorting.
**Authoritative Source**: O'Brien, D. (2010). "Tree Testing: Fast, Iterative Evaluation of Menu Labels and Categories." Boxes and Arrows. Also: Optimal Workshop documentation.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-7 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: setup + participants + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: tree testing tooling + participant recruitment or incentives + analysis time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Create the tree** – Build a text-based hierarchy of your navigation structure in a tree testing tool. Include 3-5 levels of depth. (2-4 hours)
2. **Write tasks** – Create 8-12 task scenarios: "Where would you go to [do X]?" Tasks should cover key user goals across different sections. (2-3 hours)
3. **Recruit participants** – 50-100 participants is a common practical target (a useful heuristic, not a statistical floor). Use unmoderated remote format for speed. (1-3 days)
4. **Analyze success rates** – For each task: % correct (found the right item), % incorrect, and % who gave up. Also analyze first click accuracy and path analysis. (1 day)
5. **Iterate the tree** – Investigate paths with notably low success rates (60% is a common practitioner threshold, not a universal standard). Re-test problematic branches. Compare before/after scores. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- After card sorting, to validate the proposed IA
- Before visual design, to test navigation without design bias
- When redesigning navigation and need to compare structures

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to test visual design or interaction patterns (→ usability testing)
- The IA is trivial (5 or fewer top-level items with obvious labels)
- You're testing content quality, not findability

**Output/Deliverable**: Task success rates, first-click accuracy, path analysis (where users go wrong), and IA improvement recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: quantitative IA validation, isolates structure from design
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate task scenarios from user stories and help analyze path data for common failure patterns.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M13 or draft IA structure

## METHOD 15: Five-Second Test

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Participants view a design or page for exactly 5 seconds, then answer questions about what they noticed, understood, and remember. Tests first impressions, visual hierarchy, and clarity of messaging.

**Origin**: Developed by Christine Perfetti and Jared Spool at UIE (2005). Based on research showing users form impressions within seconds.
**Authoritative Source**: Perfetti, C. (2005). "5-Second Tests: Measuring Your Site's Content Pages." UIE. Also: UsabilityHub (now Lyssna) documentation.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: stimulus selection + recall-question setup + test run + response analysis
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal setup time + lightweight testing tool use

**How to Apply**:
1. **Select the design** – Choose the page, screen, or design mockup to test. Works best for landing pages, homepages, key feature pages, and marketing materials. (1 hour)
2. **Write recall questions** – "What is this page about?", "What do you remember?", "What action would you take?", "Who is this product for?" (1 hour)
3. **Run the test** – Upload to a five-second test tool or run in person. Participant sees design for 5 seconds, then answers questions without the design visible. 20-50 participants. (1-2 days)
4. **Analyze responses** – Categorize open-ended answers. Calculate: % who correctly identified the purpose, % who noticed key elements, % who understood the value proposition. (Half day)
5. **Iterate** – If <60% correctly identify the page purpose, the visual hierarchy or messaging needs work. Redesign and re-test. (As needed)

**When to Use**:
- You want to test whether a landing page communicates its value proposition clearly
- You're choosing between design directions for first impressions
- You need quick, cheap validation of visual hierarchy and messaging

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're testing complex interactions or workflows (→ usability testing)
- The page requires reading/comprehension beyond first impressions
- You need behavioral data, not recall data

**Output/Deliverable**: Recall accuracy scores, first impression themes, value proposition clarity rating, and design iteration recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Weak-Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: measures impressions, not behavior; useful for quick directional feedback
**AI Augmentation**: AI can categorize open-ended recall responses and identify patterns across participants faster than manual coding.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 16: Usability Testing (Moderated)

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A facilitator guides participants through tasks on a product/prototype while observing their behavior, asking follow-up questions, and probing their thought process in real time. The gold standard for understanding usability issues.

**Origin**: Formalized by Jakob Nielsen and Tom Landauer (1993, "A Mathematical Model of Usability Testing"). Popularized by Steve Krug (*Don't Make Me Think*, 2000).
**Authoritative Source**: Krug, S. (2014). *Don't Make Me Think, Revisited*, 3rd Ed. New Riders. Also: Rubin, J. & Chisnell, D. (2008). *Handbook of Usability Testing*, 2nd Ed. Wiley. Nielsen, J. (2000). "Why You Only Need to Test with 5 Users." Nielsen Norman Group.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: facilitator + note-takers/observers
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: recruiting + sessions + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: participant recruitment + facilitation + synthesis + optional lab or platform support

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define test objectives** – What specific questions about usability do you need to answer? Write 3-5 research questions. (1-2 hours)
2. **Create a test plan** – Write 5-8 realistic task scenarios. Define success criteria for each task (completion, time, errors). Prepare a screening questionnaire. (1 day)
3. **Recruit participants** – 5-8 per round (five is a common starting point for qualitative rounds; the right number depends on user group diversity and study goals). Screen for your target user profile. (1-2 weeks)
4. **Conduct sessions** – 45-60 minutes each. Ask participants to think aloud. Observe without helping. Use follow-up probes: "What were you expecting?" "What would you do next?" Record screen and audio. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Analyze and prioritize findings** – Rate issues by severity (critical/major/minor) and frequency. Create a prioritized issues list. Include video clips of key moments. (1-2 days)
6. **Report and iterate** – Share findings with the team. Fix critical issues. Re-test to verify fixes. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- You're testing a new design, flow, or prototype before development
- You need to understand WHY users struggle, not just WHERE (the think-aloud reveals causation)
- Key user journeys need validation before launch

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need quantitative metrics from large samples (→ unmoderated testing)
- The design is live and you can run A/B tests instead
- You're testing value/desirability, not usability (→ concept testing or fake door)

**Output/Deliverable**: Prioritized usability issues list (severity × frequency), task success rates, think-aloud insights, and video highlight reel.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: direct observation of real behavior with rich qualitative data; small qualitative rounds surface most critical issues
**AI Augmentation**: AI can transcribe sessions, tag usability issues by type, and create highlight reels—but a human facilitator remains essential for probing and adapting in real time.

## METHOD 18: Expert/Heuristic Review

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: UX experts evaluate a product against established usability principles (heuristics) to identify potential usability issues without testing with real users. Fast and cost-effective way to find obvious problems.

**Origin**: Jakob Nielsen's 10 Usability Heuristics (1994). Also: Gerhardt-Powals' cognitive engineering principles and Weinschenk & Barker classification.
**Authoritative Source**: Nielsen, J. (1994). "10 Usability Heuristics for User Interface Design." Nielsen Norman Group. Also: Nielsen, J. & Molich, R. (1990). "Heuristic Evaluation of User Interfaces." *CHI '90 Proceedings*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo
**Team Notes**: 1-3 experts
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: scope definition + independent review + consolidation + reporting
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: expert review time + optional external specialist support

**How to Apply**:
1. **Select heuristics** – Use Nielsen's 10 heuristics or a domain-specific set. For mobile, add mobile-specific heuristics. For accessibility, add WCAG guidelines. (1 hour)
2. **Define scope** – Which screens, flows, or features to review? Prioritize key user journeys. (1 hour)
3. **Independent review** – Each expert evaluates independently (avoid groupthink). Walk through each screen against each heuristic. Document issues with severity ratings. (2-4 hours per expert)
4. **Consolidate findings** – Merge issues from all experts. Remove duplicates. Rate severity (0-4 scale: cosmetic to catastrophic). (2-4 hours)
5. **Prioritize** – Create a prioritized issues list: critical issues (fix before launch), major (fix soon), minor (fix eventually), cosmetic (nice to fix). (1-2 hours)
6. **Report** – Present findings with screenshots, heuristic violated, severity, and recommendation. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- You need fast, cheap usability feedback before user testing
- Budget or time doesn't allow for user testing
- You want to identify obvious issues so usability tests can focus on deeper problems

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're using expert review as a REPLACEMENT for user testing (experts identify many issues users would find, but miss context-dependent and task-specific ones)
- The product is in early concept stage (not enough to evaluate against heuristics)
- You don't have access to UX expertise (non-experts applying heuristics produce unreliable results)

**Output/Deliverable**: Heuristic evaluation report with prioritized issues, severity ratings, screenshots, and recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Weak-Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: expert judgment, not user evidence; useful as a complement to user testing, not a replacement
**AI Augmentation**: AI can perform a preliminary heuristic check by analyzing screenshots and identifying common pattern violations, but cannot replace expert judgment for subtle issues.

## METHOD 19: Accessibility Audit

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Systematic evaluation of a product against accessibility standards (WCAG 2.1/2.2, Section 508, EN 301 549) to ensure usability for people with disabilities. Combines automated scanning with manual expert testing.

**Origin**: Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) developed by W3C Web Accessibility Initiative, first published 1999, current version WCAG 2.2 (2023).
**Authoritative Source**: W3C. (2023). *Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.2*. https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/. Also: Krug, S. (2014). *Don't Make Me Think*. WebAIM (https://webaim.org/).

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability; secondary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: legally required in many jurisdictions for Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: automated scanning + manual keyboard and screen-reader testing + standards review + remediation planning
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: accessibility tooling + expert review time + assistive technology testing + remediation planning

**How to Apply**:
1. **Run automated scans** – Use an automated accessibility scanner to identify violations (WCAG 2.1 AA). Fix all auto-detectable issues first (automated tools catch only part of the problem space—many WCAG criteria require human judgment). (1-2 days)
2. **Manual keyboard testing** – Navigate the entire product using only a keyboard. Verify all functionality is reachable, focus indicators are visible, and tab order is logical. (1-2 days)
3. **Screen reader testing** – Test with major screen readers on target platforms. Verify all content is announced correctly, forms are labeled, and dynamic content is handled. (2-3 days)
4. **Check WCAG criteria** – Systematically evaluate against WCAG 2.2 Level AA success criteria. Document each failure with criterion reference, description, and remediation. (2-5 days)
5. **Test with assistive technology users** – If possible, include people with disabilities in testing. Their real-world experience reveals issues experts miss. (1-2 weeks)
6. **Create remediation plan** – Prioritize by impact (how many users affected) and severity (blocker vs. annoyance). Set compliance targets and deadlines. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- Legal compliance requirements (ADA, Section 508, EAA, EN 301 549)
- You're launching a new product or major redesign
- You've received accessibility complaints from users

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a checkbox exercise without commitment to fixing findings (wastes time and money)
- For internal tools with no external compliance requirements (still good practice, but lower priority)
- When you think automated tools alone are sufficient (they catch only part of the problem space—many issues require manual keyboard, screen reader, and assistive technology testing)

**Output/Deliverable**: WCAG conformance report (VPAT/ACR format), prioritized issues list, remediation plan with effort estimates.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: standards-based, legally defensible, covers broad disability categories
**AI Augmentation**: AI can assist with automated scanning and generate remediation suggestions, but manual testing with assistive technologies remains irreplaceable.

## METHOD 20: A/B Testing

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Randomly splitting users between two (or more) versions of a page, feature, or experience and measuring which performs better against a defined metric. The gold standard for causal evidence in optimization.

**Origin**: Roots in agricultural experimentation (Ronald Fisher, 1920s). Applied to web optimization by Google (2000) and popularized by Kohavi, Tang & Xu.
**Authoritative Source**: Kohavi, R., Tang, D. & Xu, Y. (2020). *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments: A Practical Guide to A/B Testing*. Cambridge University Press. Also: Kohavi, R. & Thomke, S. (2017). "The Surprising Power of Online Experiments." *Harvard Business Review*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Viability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: product + engineering + data
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: requires sufficient traffic for statistical significance
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: hypothesis design + sample-size planning + variant build and QA + experiment run + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: experimentation platform + engineering setup + analytics or data support

**How to Apply**:
1. **Form a clear hypothesis** – "Changing [X] from [current] to [variant] will improve [metric] by [expected lift] because [rationale]." No hypothesis = no experiment. (1-2 hours)
2. **Calculate sample size** – Use a power calculator. Determine minimum detectable effect (MDE), significance level (usually 0.05), and power (usually 0.80). This tells you how long to run. (1-2 hours)
3. **Build and QA variants** – Implement control and treatment. Verify correct randomization, no data leakage, and proper instrumentation. (1-5 days engineering)
4. **Run the experiment** – Let it run for the calculated duration. Avoid peeking at results before the planned end—early looks inflate false positive rates unless you use sequential testing methods. Run for at least 1-2 full business cycles. (2-6 weeks)
5. **Analyze results** – Check for statistical significance (p-value), practical significance (is the lift meaningful?), and segment-level effects. Verify no SRM (Sample Ratio Mismatch). (1-2 days)
6. **Make a decision** – Ship the winner, iterate, or investigate. Document the learning regardless of outcome. (1 day)

**When to Use**:
- You have enough traffic for a properly powered test (required sample size depends on baseline rate, minimum detectable effect, and variance—use a power calculator, not a fixed rule)
- You want causal evidence that a change improves a metric
- You're optimizing an existing experience (not testing completely new products)

**When NOT to Use**:
- Traffic is too low for statistical significance (→ use qualitative methods instead)
- You're testing fundamentally different products, not variations (→ concept testing or fake door)
- The change is obviously needed (e.g., fixing a broken button—just fix it, don't experiment)

**Output/Deliverable**: Statistical analysis with confidence intervals, lift estimates, segment breakdowns, and go/no-go decision with supporting evidence.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: causal evidence with statistical rigor—the highest evidence standard for optimization
**AI Augmentation**: AI can suggest experiment designs, detect anomalies during runs, and perform automated segment analysis—but humans must define the hypothesis and interpret business implications.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 21: Multivariate Testing

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Testing multiple variables simultaneously (e.g., headline + image + CTA button) to understand both individual and interaction effects. Finds the optimal combination of elements rather than testing one change at a time.

**Origin**: Extension of factorial experimental design (Ronald Fisher, 1920s). Applied to web optimization in the 2000s as platforms enabled it.
**Authoritative Source**: Kohavi, R., Tang, D. & Xu, Y. (2020). *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments*. Cambridge University Press. Also: Google Optimize documentation (now sunsetted; VWO/Optimizely documentation).

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: requires data science support
**Best For Company**: Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: requires very high traffic volumes
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 4-8 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: variable selection + matrix design + implementation + run + interaction analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: experimentation platform + engineering implementation + data science analysis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Identify variables** – Choose 2-4 page elements to test (headline, image, CTA, layout). Define 2-3 variations per element. (Half day)
2. **Create the test matrix** – Full factorial: all possible combinations (e.g., 3 headlines × 2 images × 2 CTAs = 12 variants). Or fractional factorial to reduce variants. (1-2 hours)
3. **Calculate required traffic** – Each variant needs sufficient traffic. Use a power calculator accounting for the number of combinations, baseline rate, and minimum detectable effect. Traffic requirements grow quickly with variant count—what works for a simple A/B test is rarely sufficient here. (1 hour)
4. **Build and launch** – Implement all variants. Use a proper multivariate testing tool. Verify correct randomization. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Run to significance** – Typically 4-8 weeks. Do not stop early. (4-8 weeks)
6. **Analyze main effects and interactions** – Which individual elements matter most? Do any combinations create unexpected synergies or conflicts? (1-3 days)

**When to Use**:
- You have very high traffic—enough to properly power all variant combinations given your baseline rate and minimum detectable effect (use a power calculator, not a fixed threshold)
- You want to optimize multiple elements of a page simultaneously
- You suspect interaction effects between elements (e.g., a certain image works better with a certain headline)

**When NOT to Use**:
- Traffic is insufficient for the number of variants (most common mistake)
- You're making a big directional change, not optimizing elements (→ A/B test first)
- You don't have data science support to analyze interaction effects properly

**Output/Deliverable**: Main effect analysis per variable, interaction effects matrix, optimal combination recommendation, and lift estimates.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: when properly powered; statistically rigorous multi-variable optimization
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help design fractional factorial experiments, identify significant interactions, and recommend optimal combinations from complex result sets.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 22: Switchback Testing

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Alternating between treatment and control conditions over time periods (e.g., hour by hour, day by day) for the same population, rather than splitting users. Used when user-level randomization isn't possible or when there are network effects.

**Origin**: Developed to address situations where user-level randomization is invalid because treatment effects propagate across users or time—particularly common in marketplace, logistics, and platform contexts where supply and demand are interdependent.
**Authoritative Source**: Bojinov, I. et al. (2020). "Design and Analysis of Switchback Experiments." MIT Sloan. Also: Uber Engineering Blog, "Switchback Tests and Randomized Experimentation Under Network Effects."

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: engineering + data science
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: any product where user-level randomization is invalid due to network effects or market-level interference
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: window design + randomization setup + live run + clustered analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: engineering implementation + experimentation infrastructure + data science analysis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Determine if switchback is needed** – Is user-level randomization impossible due to network/marketplace effects? If yes, switchback is appropriate. (Decision point)
2. **Design time windows** – Define alternation periods (e.g., 1-hour blocks, daily). Shorter periods reduce confounding but increase switching costs. Balance precision vs. practicality. (1-2 days)
3. **Randomize time periods** – Randomly assign each time window to treatment or control. Ensure balanced allocation across days-of-week and times-of-day. (1 day)
4. **Implement and run** – Build the switching mechanism. Run for 2-4 weeks minimum to cover variation across weekdays/weekends and other cycles. (2-6 weeks)
5. **Analyze with appropriate methods** – Standard A/B test statistics don't apply. Use cluster-randomized analysis or bootstrap methods accounting for temporal correlation. (2-5 days)

**When to Use**:
- Marketplace or platform products where one user's experience affects others
- Pricing or algorithm changes that affect the entire market simultaneously
- Logistics/delivery optimization where supply and demand are interconnected

**When NOT to Use**:
- Standard user-level A/B testing is feasible (switchback is unnecessarily complex)
- The treatment has lingering effects across time periods (carryover effects contaminate results)
- You don't have data science capability to analyze clustered temporal data

**Output/Deliverable**: Treatment effect estimates with temporal clustering accounted for, analysis of carryover effects, and business impact projections.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: addresses network effects that standard A/B testing can't handle, but complex to analyze correctly
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help design optimal switching schedules and perform the complex statistical analysis required, including detecting carryover effects.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 23: Fake Door / Painted Door Test

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Presenting a feature, product, or option that doesn't actually exist yet (a button, menu item, or CTA) and measuring how many users click/engage with it. Measures real demand with zero build cost.

**Origin**: Lean Startup tradition (Eric Ries, 2011). "Fake door," "painted door," and "feature fake" are common practitioner terms for this technique—widely used but not a formally standardized method with a single canonical lineage.
**Authoritative Source**: Ries, E. (2011). *The Lean Startup*. Crown Business. Also: Croll, A. & Yoskovitz, B. (2013). *Lean Analytics*. O'Reilly. Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes (in-product variant); No (campaign or landing-page variant)
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: build + exposure + measurement
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: minimal implementation + analytics setup

**How to Apply**:
1. **Identify the hypothesis** – "X% of users want [feature/product]." Define the minimum click-through rate that would validate demand. (1-2 hours)
2. **Create the fake door** – Add a button, menu item, link, or CTA that represents the feature. It should look real and be placed where users would naturally discover it. (1-2 days engineering)
3. **Design the reveal** – When clicked, show a message: "Thanks for your interest! This feature is coming soon. Want to be notified?" Collect email or just log the click. Avoid frustrating users. (1-2 hours)
4. **Expose and measure** – Run for 1-2 weeks or until you have statistically meaningful click data. Track: click rate, unique users who clicked, user segment, and repeat clicks. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Analyze and decide** – Compare click rate to your threshold. If above threshold, you have demand signal. If below, reconsider the feature or its positioning. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- You want to validate demand for a feature before building it
- You need quantitative evidence to justify development investment
- The feature is expensive to build and you want to de-risk before committing

**When NOT to Use**:
- The feature is small enough that it's faster to just build it and measure usage
- Your users will be frustrated or lose trust from fake features (carefully manage the reveal)
- You need to understand WHY people want the feature, not just IF (→ combine with interviews)

**Output/Deliverable**: Click-through rate, demand signal (above/below threshold), interested user list (for follow-up research), and go/no-go recommendation.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real behavioral signal with zero build cost, but clicking ≠ sustained usage
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help design the fake door copy and analyze click patterns by segment, but the method itself is fundamentally simple.

**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 24: Landing Page Test

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Creating a standalone landing page for a product or feature that doesn't exist yet, driving traffic to it, and measuring conversion (email signups, pre-registrations, purchases). Tests demand and value proposition messaging simultaneously.

**Origin**: Lean Startup methodology. Popularized by Buffer's transparent validation (Joel Gascoigne, 2011) and Dropbox's explainer video MVP.
**Authoritative Source**: Ries, E. (2011). *The Lean Startup*. Crown Business. Also: Maurya, A. (2012). *Running Lean*. O'Reilly. Blank, S. (2013). *The Startup Owner's Manual*. K&S Ranch.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Startup, Scaleup
**Applicability Notes**: fast validation of new concepts
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: build page + drive traffic + measure
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: page build + traffic acquisition + analytics + follow-up with converters

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the value proposition** – Write a clear, compelling description of what the product does and why it matters. Create 2-3 messaging variants if unsure. (Half day)
2. **Build the landing page** – Use a landing page builder or no-code tool. Include: headline, value prop, key benefits, social proof (if available), and CTA (sign up, pre-order, join waitlist). (1-3 days)
3. **Set up analytics** – Track: page visits, scroll depth, CTA clicks, form completions, and conversion rate. Use web analytics and a heatmap tool. (2-4 hours)
4. **Drive traffic** – Use paid ads, community channels, or targeted outreach. Aim for 500-2,000 visitors. Budget $200-1,000 for ads. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Measure conversion** – Calculate: visitor-to-signup rate. Conversion rates vary widely by industry, traffic source, audience intent, and offer type—define your success threshold before driving traffic, and use comparable industry benchmarks where available rather than universal averages. Compare messaging variants. (After traffic period)
6. **Follow up with converters** – Email or interview people who signed up. Understand their expectations and willingness to pay. (1 week)

**When to Use**:
- You want to test if a concept has market demand before building
- You're validating positioning and messaging alongside demand
- You need to build an early waitlist for launch

**When NOT to Use**:
- Your product is complex and can't be explained on a single page
- You need to test usability or feasibility, not demand
- You're in an enterprise B2B context where landing pages don't match the buying process (→ pre-sales/LOI instead)

**Output/Deliverable**: Conversion rates by channel and variant, email waitlist, messaging effectiveness data, and demand validation.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real behavioral signal—people gave their email—but gap between signup and purchase
**AI Augmentation**: AI can write landing page copy variants, analyze visitor behavior patterns, and help optimize conversion through headline/CTA suggestions.

**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-14 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: format selection + artifact creation + exposure + measurement
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: lightweight artifact creation + distribution + measurement

**How to Apply**:
1. **Choose the smoke test format** – Video (Dropbox style), landing page, fake door, ad campaign, social media post, or email to existing users. Match format to your audience and channel. (2-4 hours)
2. **Define the success metric** – What conversion rate, click rate, or response rate would validate demand? Set the threshold BEFORE running the test. (1 hour)
3. **Create the minimum artifact** – Build the lightest possible version. A Loom video, a Carrd page, a mockup screenshot in an email, or a tweet describing the concept. (1-3 days)
4. **Expose to real audience** – Share with target users through appropriate channels. Track engagement. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Measure against threshold** – Did you hit the pre-defined success criteria? Analyze by segment. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Very early stage idea validation when you want maximum learning with minimum build
- You want to compare demand across multiple concepts quickly
- Budget and time are extremely constrained

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to test the actual product experience, not just demand
- Your audience requires hands-on experience to evaluate (complex B2B tools)
- The concept requires too much context to explain in a smoke test format

**Output/Deliverable**: Engagement/conversion metrics, demand signal, and audience interest data.
**Evidence Strength**: Weak-Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: behavioral intent signal, but low-fidelity artifacts may not capture true demand

## METHOD 26: Concierge MVP

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Manually delivering the product or service to real customers using human effort instead of technology. The core value proposition is real, but the delivery mechanism is manual. Tests whether the solution solves a real problem worth paying for.

**Origin**: Coined by Eric Ries (*The Lean Startup*, 2011). Classic example: Food on the Table (Manuel Rosso) where the founder personally created meal plans for each customer.
**Authoritative Source**: Ries, E. (2011). *The Lean Startup*. Crown Business. Also: Alvarez, C. (2014). *Lean Customer Development*. O'Reilly. Blank, S. (2013). *The Startup Owner's Manual*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: requires hands-on service delivery
**Best For Company**: Startup, Scaleup
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: recruit users + deliver service manually + iterate
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: primarily researcher or founder time; may charge participants to offset costs; no major external spend

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the core value proposition** – What specific job are you doing for the user? Strip away all technology and describe the human service. (Half day)
2. **Recruit initial customers** – Find 5-10 real target users who have the problem. Personal outreach, not ads. Explain you're offering a manual service to validate the concept. (1-2 weeks)
3. **Deliver manually** – Perform the service yourself. Use spreadsheets, email, phone calls, whatever it takes. Focus on delivering the outcome, not the experience. (2-4 weeks)
4. **Observe and iterate** – Pay attention to what customers actually use vs. what they ignore. What questions do they ask? Where do they get confused? What delights them? (Ongoing during delivery)
5. **Measure retention and willingness to pay** – Do customers come back? Would they pay? How much? At what frequency? (After 2-4 weeks)
6. **Identify automation opportunities** – What parts of the manual process are most painful to deliver and most valued by customers? These are what you should build. (Final analysis)

**When to Use**:
- You need to validate both the problem and solution before investing in technology
- The service is complex enough that you need to learn what users actually need
- You want to test willingness to pay before building

**When NOT to Use**:
- The value proposition requires technology that can't be replicated manually (e.g., real-time recommendations)
- You can't deliver meaningful value with manual processes
- The market requires scale to work (network effects, marketplace dynamics)

**Output/Deliverable**: Retention data, willingness-to-pay validation, deep qualitative understanding of user needs, and feature prioritization based on actual usage.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real service delivered to real users with real outcomes—strong behavioral signal on value and willingness to pay, though it does not validate scalability, reliability, or the eventual automated product experience
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help automate parts of the manual delivery (drafting emails, generating recommendations), but the human touch is the point of this method.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 27: Wizard of Oz

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Users interact with what appears to be a working product, but behind the scenes, humans are manually performing the operations. Unlike concierge MVP, users typically interact through a product interface rather than directly with the team—and concealment is common, though not always required. Tests the complete user experience without building the technology.

**Origin**: Named after the film (man behind the curtain). Term coined in HCI by Jeff Kelley (1983, Johns Hopkins University) for speech interface research.
**Authoritative Source**: Kelley, J.F. (1984). "An Iterative Design Methodology for User-Friendly Natural Language Office Information Applications." *ACM Transactions on Information Systems*. Also: Ries, E. (2011). *The Lean Startup*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Startup, Scaleup
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: build facade + manual operation + observation
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: facade build costs + manual operation time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Build the facade** – Create a realistic front-end (UI, chatbot interface, or product surface) that users interact with normally. (1-2 weeks)
2. **Set up the "wizard" operation** – Define the manual process behind the scenes. Who responds? How quickly? Using what tools? Create an operations playbook. (2-3 days)
3. **Recruit and onboard users** – Get 5-20 real users to use the "product." Concealment is common (users unaware the system is manually operated), but transparency about the simulation is also a valid approach depending on context and ethics. (1 week)
4. **Operate and observe** – Run the manual operations. Track response times, edge cases, and user behavior. Document everything. (2-4 weeks)
5. **Measure the experience** – Satisfaction, task completion, repeat usage, and willingness to continue. Does the experience work? (After 2-4 weeks)
6. **Debrief and plan** – Analyze what you learned. Which parts need automation? What user behaviors were unexpected? What's the minimum viable technology? (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- You want to test the full user experience including the interface, not just the value proposition
- The technology is complex (AI, ML, algorithms) and you want to validate the concept before investing in it
- You need to test natural-feeling interactions (chatbots, recommendations, search)

**When NOT to Use**:
- Ethical concerns about deceiving users (consider disclosure or concierge MVP instead)
- The manual operation can't match the expected response time (e.g., real-time applications)
- You can build a working prototype faster than operating the facade manually

**Output/Deliverable**: User experience validation, behavioral data, edge cases discovered, automation requirements, and feasibility assessment.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: users interact with a simulated product experience; behavior reflects response to the interface and value proposition, though authenticity depends on how realistic the facade is
**AI Augmentation**: AI can partially automate the "wizard" role (drafting responses for human review), reducing manual effort while maintaining the facade.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 28: Paper Prototyping

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Hand-drawn sketches of screens and UI elements used to test concepts, flows, and layouts with users before any digital design work. The fastest, cheapest way to explore design ideas and test with real users.

**Origin**: Popularized by Carolyn Snyder (2003). Used since the earliest days of HCI and interface design.
**Authoritative Source**: Snyder, C. (2003). *Paper Prototyping: The Fast and Easy Way to Design and Refine User Interfaces*. Morgan Kaufmann. Also: Buxton, B. (2007). *Sketching User Experiences*. Morgan Kaufmann.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: sketch + test with 3-5 users
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal time only – paper, markers, and participant time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Sketch key screens** – Draw the main screens on paper or index cards. Include enough detail for users to understand (buttons, text areas, navigation) but keep it rough. Rough = faster iteration and less attachment. (1-3 hours)
2. **Prepare the interaction** – Cut out movable elements (dropdown menus, dialog boxes, error messages). Create a "screen stack" so you can swap screens as the user "navigates." (1-2 hours)
3. **Write task scenarios** – "You want to [goal]. Where would you start?" 5-8 tasks covering key flows. (1 hour)
4. **Test with users** – You act as the "computer," swapping screens in response to user actions. One person facilitates, another plays the computer. Ask think-aloud. (30-45 min per participant, 3-5 participants)
5. **Iterate between sessions** – Redraw screens that caused confusion. Test new versions with subsequent participants. (15-30 min between sessions)

**When to Use**:
- Very early in the design process when you're exploring multiple concepts
- You want to iterate rapidly without investing in digital tools
- You want to test information architecture, content labels, basic flows, and broad interaction concepts—not visual design

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to test detailed interactions, animations, or responsive behavior
- Users expect a realistic experience and paper would break immersion
- The design is far enough along that digital prototyping is more efficient

**Output/Deliverable**: Validated (or invalidated) design concepts, initial flow improvements, and key usability insights from early-stage testing.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: real user feedback on concepts, but low fidelity may not reveal all usability issues
**AI Augmentation**: Limited—paper prototyping is inherently a physical, hands-on activity. AI can help generate wireframe sketches to print and test.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 29: Digital Prototyping

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Creating interactive mockups using design and prototyping tools – including AI-assisted prototyping – that simulate the product experience without writing code. Ranges from clickable wireframes (low fidelity) to pixel-perfect interactive designs (high fidelity).

**Origin**: Rooted in interactive prototyping practice and fidelity theory in HCI (Houde & Hill, 1997; Buxton, 2007). The core idea—matching prototype fidelity to the question being answered—predates modern tools. Contemporary digital tools (Figma, Axure, Framer) have lowered the cost and raised the ceiling of what teams can prototype without code.
**Authoritative Source**: Warfel, T.Z. (2009). *Prototyping: A Practitioner's Guide*. Rosenfeld Media. Also: Houde, S. & Hill, C. (1997). "What do Prototypes Prototype?" in Helander, M. et al., *Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction*. Elsevier. Buxton, B. (2007). *Sketching User Experiences*. Morgan Kaufmann (Chapter on fidelity spectrum).

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: designer + researcher
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-10 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: design + testing
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: designer time + prototyping tooling + participant incentives for testing

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define fidelity level** – Low-fi (wireframes, grayscale, placeholder content) for early exploration. High-fi (real content, visual design, animations) for validation before development. Match fidelity to the question you're answering. (Decision)
2. **Build key flows** – Prototype the 3-5 most important user journeys. Don't prototype everything—focus on what you need to test. (2-5 days)
3. **Add interactions** – Make it clickable: buttons link to screens, forms show states, navigation works. Use your design tool's prototyping features. (1-2 days)
4. **Test with users** – Run moderated or unmoderated usability tests using the prototype. 5-8 participants. Share via prototype link. (3-7 days)
5. **Iterate** – Fix issues in the design tool (minutes vs. days in code). Re-test if needed. (1-3 days)

**When to Use**:
- You need to test interactions, flows, and visual design before building
- Stakeholders need to "see it" before committing to development
- You want to test with users at a higher fidelity than paper but without code

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're testing value/demand, not usability (→ fake door or landing page is faster)
- The prototype would take as long as building the real thing
- You're spending weeks on pixel-perfect prototypes when wireframes would suffice (over-investing in fidelity)

**Output/Deliverable**: Interactive prototype, usability test results, validated design ready for development, and design specification.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: realistic enough to surface real usability issues, but users still know it's not "real"
**AI Augmentation**: AI tools (Galileo AI, Uizard) can generate initial wireframes and layouts, accelerating the starting point. AI can also generate placeholder content.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 30: Pretotyping

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Superseded
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended

**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Testing the core premise of a product idea with the absolute minimum investment—often before even a prototype. "Fake it before you make it." Tests whether people would use it, not whether you can build it.

**Origin**: Alberto Savoia, former Google Engineering Director. Coined "pretotyping" (before prototyping). Published 2011.
**Authoritative Source**: Savoia, A. (2019). *The Right It: Why So Many Ideas Fail and How to Make Sure Yours Succeed*. HarperOne. Also: Savoia, A. (2011). "Pretotype It" (free ebook).

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Startup, Scaleup
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-7 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: hypothesis definition + pretotype artifact creation + market exposure + behavioral measurement
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: minimal artifact creation, distribution effort

**How to Apply**:
1. **State the hypothesis** – "At least X% of [target users] will [desired behavior] within [timeframe]." Be specific and falsifiable. (1 hour)
2. **Choose a pretotype technique** – Mechanical Turk (human-powered facade), Pinocchio (non-functional physical model), Fake Door, Impersonator (existing tool used differently), Provincial (limited market test), One-Night Stand (brief real experience). (1 hour)
3. **Build in hours, not days** – Create the minimum artifact. Savoia's rule: if building takes more than a day, you're building too much. (1 day max)
4. **Expose to real market** – Put it in front of real potential users in a real context. Track YODA (Your Own Data Ain't lying): actual behavior, not opinions. (1-5 days)
5. **Apply the XYZ Hypothesis** – Did X% of target users exhibit behavior Y within timeframe Z? If yes, proceed. If no, pivot or kill. (1 hour)

**When to Use**:
- You have many ideas and need to quickly filter for the ones worth investing in
- You want to test the fundamental premise before any design or engineering work
- You need data to convince stakeholders to invest in exploration

**When NOT to Use**:
- You've already validated demand and need to test usability or feasibility
- The idea requires significant context to evaluate (complex B2B enterprise solutions)
- You're in a regulated space where even pretotypes might have compliance implications

**Output/Deliverable**: XYZ hypothesis result (validated/invalidated), YODA data (real behavioral metrics), and go/no-go decision.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real behavioral data, but extremely low fidelity—measures interest, not sustained engagement
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help generate pretotype concepts, draft landing page copy, and create the minimal artifacts needed for testing.
**Superseded By**: M23, M24
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 31: Design Sprint

**Category**: Collaborative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: A structured 5-day process for rapidly prototyping and testing ideas. Monday: map the problem. Tuesday: sketch solutions. Wednesday: decide. Thursday: prototype. Friday: test with real users (5 is a common sprint convention, not a fixed rule – the evidence comes from the Friday test, not the sprint itself). Compresses months of work into one week.

**Origin**: Developed by Jake Knapp at Google Ventures (GV), drawing on IDEO's design thinking and Lean Startup methodology.
**Authoritative Source**: Knapp, J., Zeratsky, J. & Kowitz, B. (2016). *Sprint: How to Solve Big Problems and Test New Ideas in Just Five Days*. Simon & Schuster.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Feasibility
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: 5-7 people for a full week
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: but ROI is highest for significant initiatives that justify 5-7 people × 5 days
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 5 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: prep + map + sketch + decide + prototype + test
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: full-team time commitment + facilitation + prototype effort

**How to Apply**:
1. **Monday – Map** – Define the challenge. Create a target map (user journey + goal). Interview internal experts. Choose a specific target to focus the week. (Full day)
2. **Tuesday – Sketch** – Individual brainstorming. Lightning demos of existing solutions. Four-step sketching: notes → ideas → Crazy 8s → solution sketch. Each person creates a detailed solution concept. (Full day)
3. **Wednesday – Decide** – Silent voting on sketches. Group discussion. Decider makes the call on which concept(s) to prototype. Create a storyboard for Friday's prototype. (Full day)
4. **Thursday – Prototype** – Build a realistic-looking prototype (design tool, slides, or even a physical facade). Divide labor: makers, writers, asset collectors, stitcher. "Goldilocks quality"—just enough to test. (Full day)
5. **Friday – Test** – Run 5 one-on-one usability tests with real target users. Watch together (remote observation). Debrief after each session. Identify patterns. (Full day)
6. **Post-sprint – Decide next steps** – What did you learn? Build it? Iterate? Kill it? Run another sprint? (Half day, following Monday)

**When to Use**:
- Big, ambiguous challenges where the team doesn't know where to start
- Cross-functional alignment is needed alongside solution exploration
- You want to compress months of debate into one decisive week

**When NOT to Use**:
- The question is small enough that a 2-hour assumption mapping would suffice (→ don't burn 5-7 person-weeks on a feature tweak)
- You already know what to build and need to test it (→ just prototype and test)
- Team can't commit 5 full days (half-hearted sprints produce half-hearted results)

**Output/Deliverable**: Tested prototype, user feedback from 5 sessions, validated/invalidated concept, and clear next steps (build/iterate/kill).
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: rapid concept validation with real users, but 5 users and 1 prototype is a starting point, not definitive evidence
**AI Augmentation**: AI can assist with research compilation for Monday, generate sketch variations for Tuesday, help write prototype content for Thursday, and analyze test recordings from Friday.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 32: Assumption Mapping

**Category**: Strategic Framing
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: Systematically identifying, categorizing, and prioritizing the assumptions underlying a product idea. Maps assumptions by importance and uncertainty to focus discovery efforts where they matter most.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – it directs which discovery methods to run but does not generate evidence itself. Using Assumption Mapping in place of actual research is Discovery Karaoke; using it to focus your research is exactly the right application.

**Origin**: Developed in the Lean Startup and design thinking communities. Formalized by David Bland and Alex Osterwalder.
**Authoritative Source**: Bland, D. & Osterwalder, A. (2020). *Testing Business Ideas*. Wiley. Also: Gothelf, J. & Seiden, J. (2016). *Lean UX*. Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits*.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Feasibility, Viability, Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5), Cross-functional (5+)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 hours
**Time Order**: Hours
**Time Notes**: assumption listing + risk tagging + mapping + prioritization + experiment design
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal workshop time + whiteboard or digital collaboration tool

**How to Apply**:
1. **List all assumptions** – Brainstorm every assumption your product/feature relies on. "We assume users will…", "We assume the market…", "We assume we can…". Aim for 20-40 assumptions across all risk types. (30-60 minutes)
2. **Categorize by risk type** – Tag each as Value, Usability, Feasibility, Viability, or Compliance. This reveals which risk types you have the most untested assumptions about. (15-20 minutes)
3. **Map on 2×2 matrix** – X-axis: Evidence (no evidence → strong evidence). Y-axis: Importance (if wrong, minimal impact → if wrong, fatal). Plot each assumption. (20-30 minutes)
4. **Prioritize the "leap of faith" assumptions** – Top-right quadrant: high importance + low evidence = your riskiest assumptions. These are what discovery should focus on. (10 minutes)
5. **Design experiments for top assumptions** – For each leap-of-faith assumption, choose the right discovery method based on the risk type. (30-60 minutes)

**When to Use**:
- Starting any new initiative and you need to focus discovery efforts
- Team is debating what to research first (assumption mapping creates clarity)
- You want to avoid the "research everything" trap and focus on what matters most

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're past the assumption stage and need to run experiments (→ just run them)
- The initiative is so small that listing assumptions is overkill (→ just build and measure)
- The team isn't willing to honestly assess their own uncertainty (cognitive bias blocks the exercise)

**Output/Deliverable**: Prioritized assumption map, top 3-5 "leap of faith" assumptions, and a discovery plan targeting the highest-risk unknowns.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: meta-method – it doesn't generate evidence directly, it focuses your efforts on generating the right evidence
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate initial assumption lists from product briefs, identify hidden assumptions the team missed, and suggest experiments for each assumption type.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 33: Opportunity Solution Trees (OST)

**Category**: Strategic Framing
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: A visual framework connecting desired outcomes to discovered opportunities to potential solutions to experiments. Ensures solutions are grounded in real user opportunities and systematically tested.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – it structures and visualises the relationships between outcomes, opportunities, and solutions to guide discovery priorities, but does not generate evidence on its own. Its value is in making sure the right methods are run on the right questions.

**Origin**: Developed by Teresa Torres as part of the Continuous Discovery Habits framework.
**Authoritative Source**: Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits: Discover Products that Create Customer Value and Business Value*. Product Talk. Also: producttalk.org

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability, Feasibility
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: product trio (PM, designer, engineer)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: particularly effective for Scaleup and Enterprise maintaining continuous discovery
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: Ongoing
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: initial tree setup + opportunity mapping + solution generation + experiment updates + weekly review
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal trio time + whiteboard or digital collaboration tool

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the outcome** – Start with a clear, measurable business/product outcome. "Increase activation rate from 30% to 45%." This is the root of the tree. (1 hour)
2. **Map opportunities from research** – From user interviews and data, identify the user needs, pain points, and desires related to this outcome. These are "opportunity nodes." Organize them hierarchically. (1-2 days)
3. **Generate solutions** – For each opportunity, brainstorm 3+ possible solutions. This ensures you're comparing alternatives, not defaulting to the first idea. (Half day)
4. **Design assumption tests** – For each solution, identify the riskiest assumption and design a small experiment to test it. Use the assumption mapping approach. (Half day per solution)
5. **Run experiments and update the tree** – Test, learn, update. Mark validated opportunities. Prune invalidated branches. Add new opportunities as you learn. (Ongoing)
6. **Review weekly** – The product trio reviews the tree weekly, incorporating new research, updating experiments, and making decisions. (1 hour per week)

**When to Use**:
- You want to establish a continuous discovery practice (not just one-off research)
- The team needs a structured way to connect user research to product decisions
- You have multiple possible solutions and need to systematically compare them

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're doing a one-time research project, not continuous discovery
- The problem is so simple that a tree is unnecessary overhead
- The team isn't doing regular user research to feed opportunities into the tree

**Output/Deliverable**: Visual opportunity solution tree showing outcomes → opportunities → solutions → experiments, updated continuously.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: meta-framework – its strength depends on the quality of research and experiments within it
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help brainstorm solution alternatives for opportunities, suggest experiments, and identify gaps in the tree where more research is needed.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M1 or M60

**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: research synthesis + journey drafting + cross-functional review
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: research synthesis time + facilitation + workshop alignment

**How to Apply**:
1. **Choose the scope** – Which persona? Which journey? (Awareness → Purchase → Onboarding → Regular Use → Support → Renewal/Churn). Define start and end points. (1-2 hours)

- The journey is simple and well-understood (→ unnecessary overhead)

**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Complex

**Team Notes**: needs representation from all departments involved

## METHOD 37: Empathy Mapping

**Category**: Synthesis
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Synthesis Tool
**Short Description**: A structured visual template capturing what a user says, thinks, does, and feels about a topic or experience. Creates shared empathy within the team and surfaces contradictions between stated and actual behavior.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – Empathy Maps synthesise and communicate what the team knows (or assumes) about users' thoughts, feelings, and actions. They do not generate new evidence. A map filled with team assumptions instead of research data is a projection exercise, not an empathy exercise. Use after real research; use gaps to plan further discovery.

**Origin**: Developed by Dave Gray at XPLANE (now Dachis Group). Published in *Gamestorming* (2010).
**Authoritative Source**: Gray, D., Brown, S. & Macanufo, J. (2010). *Gamestorming: A Playbook for Innovators, Rulebreakers, and Changemakers*. O'Reilly. Also: Gibbons, S. (2018). "Empathy Mapping." Nielsen Norman Group.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Small (2-5), Cross-functional (5+)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 hours
**Time Order**: Hours
**Time Notes**: segment selection + quadrant synthesis + contradiction review + insight capture
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal workshop time + whiteboard or digital collaboration tool

**How to Apply**:
1. **Choose the user segment** – One empathy map per persona/segment. Be specific about who you're mapping. (5 minutes)
2. **Fill in the four quadrants** – Based on research data (not assumptions): SAYS (direct quotes from interviews), THINKS (what they're really thinking, even if not said), DOES (observable behaviors and actions), FEELS (emotional state—frustrated, anxious, hopeful). (30-45 minutes)
3. **Identify contradictions** – Where does what users SAY conflict with what they DO? These contradictions are gold—they reveal real needs vs. stated preferences. (10-15 minutes)
4. **Add pains and gains** – Below the quadrants, list: Pains (fears, frustrations, obstacles) and Gains (wants, needs, measures of success). (15-20 minutes)
5. **Derive insights** – What did we learn? What surprises us? What should we investigate further? (15-20 minutes)

**When to Use**:
- You've done user research and want to build team-wide empathy
- You're starting a design workshop and need to align the team on user perspective
- You want a quick synthesis activity to organize interview data

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're filling it in from assumptions, not research (→ garbage in, garbage out)
- You're using it as a substitute for actual user research
- The team has strong user empathy already and this would be redundant

**Output/Deliverable**: Empathy map(s) showing says/thinks/does/feels quadrants with pains and gains, plus key insights.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: synthesis and alignment tool; no new evidence generated
**AI Augmentation**: AI can pre-populate empathy maps from interview transcripts, grouping quotes into the four quadrants for team review and refinement.

**Prerequisites**: M1 or M3

**Authoritative Source**: Cooper, A. (1999). *The Inmates Are Running the Asylum*. Sams Publishing. Also: Goodwin, K. (2009). *Designing for the Digital Age*. Wiley. Pruitt, J. & Adlin, T. (2006). *The Persona Lifecycle*. Morgan Kaufmann.

**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: research review + behavioral clustering + profile creation + stakeholder review
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: research synthesis + documentation + stakeholder review

**How to Apply**:
1. **Conduct user research** – Interview users across your target segments – 15-30 is a commonly cited practical range, but the right number depends on segment diversity and how many distinct behavioral patterns emerge. 2. **Identify behavioral patterns** – Cluster users by shared goals, behaviors, and contexts—not demographics. Look for 3-5 distinct patterns. (1-2 days)

- Product decisions are driven by stakeholder opinions instead of user needs
- You need to communicate user research findings in an accessible, memorable format

**When NOT to Use**:
- Personas are based on assumptions, not research (→ do the research first or skip personas)
- The team already has strong, research-backed user understanding
- You're creating personas as a deliverable that no one will reference (→ waste of time)

## METHOD 39: Value Proposition Canvas

**Category**: Strategic Framing
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: A structured tool for designing products that match customer needs. Maps customer jobs, pains, and gains on one side, and product features, pain relievers, and gain creators on the other. Helps teams articulate and test value-fit hypotheses before committing to a direction.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – the Value Proposition Canvas maps the fit between customer jobs/pains/gains and your product's features/pain relievers/gain creators. It is a synthesis and alignment tool, not an evidence-generation method. Its assumptions must be tested with methods from the Evaluative and Experimentation categories.

**Origin**: Alexander Osterwalder and Yves Pigneur, as an extension of the Business Model Canvas.
**Authoritative Source**: Osterwalder, A. et al. (2014). *Value Proposition Design*. Wiley. Also: Strategyzer.com tools and resources.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 hours (initial canvas) + ongoing refinement
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: customer-profile mapping + value-map drafting + fit assessment + validation loop
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal workshop time + template use + prior customer research input

**How to Apply**:
1. **Map the Customer Profile (right side)** – List customer jobs (functional, social, emotional), pains (obstacles, risks, undesired outcomes), and gains (desired outcomes, benefits, aspirations). Base on research, not assumptions. (1-2 hours)
2. **Map the Value Map (left side)** – List your products/services, pain relievers (how you address each pain), and gain creators (how you deliver each gain). (1-2 hours)
3. **Assess fit** – Draw connections between pains ↔ pain relievers and gains ↔ gain creators. Where do you have strong fit? Where are gaps? (30 minutes)
4. **Identify the strongest value proposition** – Which pain-reliever or gain-creator combinations are most compelling? Where do you have unique advantage? (30 minutes)
5. **Test with customers** – Present the value proposition to real users. Does it resonate? Use interviews, landing pages, or concept tests to validate. (1-2 weeks)
6. **Iterate** – Update the canvas based on test results. Kill weak value propositions. Double down on strong ones. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- You're defining or refining your product's value proposition
- You need to align product features with validated customer needs
- You're evaluating multiple value proposition options

**When NOT to Use**:
- You haven't done customer research (filling from assumptions = fiction)
- The value proposition is well-established and you're optimizing, not rethinking
- You're using it as a one-time exercise without follow-up validation

**Output/Deliverable**: Completed Value Proposition Canvas showing customer-product fit, gap analysis, and prioritized value propositions to test.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: planning tool; strength depends entirely on the quality of input customer data
**AI Augmentation**: AI can suggest customer jobs/pains/gains from market research data and identify potential value proposition gaps.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M1 or M2

## METHOD 42: Competitive Analysis

**Category**: Strategic Framing
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Systematic evaluation of competitors' products, positioning, features, pricing, and strategies. Identifies market gaps, differentiation opportunities, and standard expectations. Not about copying—about understanding the landscape.
**Context Tool Note**: Competitive Analysis maps the existing landscape – it generates evidence about the market and competitors, but does not generate evidence about your own users' needs or your product's fit. Use it to calibrate discovery scope and set realistic benchmarks; follow with methods that test your own product's assumptions.

**Origin**: Michael Porter's competitive strategy framework (1980). Adapted extensively in product management.
**Authoritative Source**: Porter, M. (1980). *Competitive Strategy*. Free Press. Also: Cagan, M. (2018). *Inspired*. Wiley (Chapter on competitive analysis in product context).

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: competitor definition + feature/positioning scan + gap analysis + strategic synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: research time + optional intelligence tooling + review of public sources

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define competitors** – Direct (same solution, same customer), indirect (different solution, same problem), and adjacent (different market, similar technology). List 5-15. (2-4 hours)
2. **Build a feature matrix** – Map key capabilities across competitors. Include pricing tiers, integrations, and platform support. (1-2 days)
3. **Analyze positioning** – How does each competitor describe themselves? What messaging and value props do they lead with? Map positioning statements. (half day)
4. **Identify gaps and opportunities** – Where are all competitors weak? Where are underserved segments? What table-stakes features does everyone have? (half day)
5. **Assess strategic trajectory** – Read funding announcements, job postings, patent filings, and product changelogs. Where are they heading? (half day)
6. **Synthesize implications** – What does this mean for your product strategy? Document "must-have" features, differentiation opportunities, and market timing implications. (half day)

**When to Use**:
- You're entering a new market or launching a new product
- You need to justify differentiation to stakeholders or investors
- You haven't refreshed competitive intelligence in 6+ months

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're using it to copy competitors rather than differentiate from them
- You're in a genuinely new category with no direct competition (→ focus on alternative solutions instead)
- Analysis paralysis: spending weeks comparing when you should be testing with users

**Output/Deliverable**: Competitive landscape document with feature matrix, positioning map, gap analysis, and strategic implications for your product.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: good for understanding the landscape; does not validate whether customers want YOUR solution
**AI Augmentation**: AI can scrape and summarize competitor websites, analyze review platforms at scale, and identify feature patterns across competitors.

## METHOD 44: Spike Solution

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A time-boxed technical investigation to answer a specific feasibility question. Throw-away code designed to learn, not to ship. The engineering equivalent of a discovery experiment.

**Origin**: Extreme Programming (XP) methodology. Kent Beck introduced spikes as a way to reduce technical uncertainty.
**Authoritative Source**: Beck, K. (2004). *Extreme Programming Explained*. Addison-Wesley. Also: Cohn, M. (2005). *Agile Estimating and Planning*. Prentice Hall.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Feasibility
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: question framing + timebox setup + minimal build + findings + decision
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: engineering time only

**How to Apply**:
1. **Frame the question** – Write one specific feasibility question: "Can we integrate with X API in under 200ms?" or "Can our database handle 10M rows with this query pattern?" (30 minutes)
2. **Set a timebox** – 1-3 days maximum. If you can't answer the question in that time, the question is too broad. (5 minutes)
3. **Build the minimum to answer** – Build only what's needed to answer the question. Minimize investment: skip polish, production architecture, and thorough documentation. The goal is learning, not shipping. The key risk is the spike silently becoming production code—keep it disposable. (1-3 days)
4. **Document findings** – What did you learn? What's possible? What's not? What are the constraints? (1-2 hours)
5. **Make the decision** – Based on findings: proceed (feasible), modify approach (feasible with changes), or kill (not feasible). (30 minutes)
6. **Throw away the code** – Spike code is NOT production code. Start fresh with proper architecture when you build for real. (Immediate)

**When to Use**:
- You have a specific technical uncertainty blocking a go/no-go decision
- The team is debating feasibility without data
- You need to estimate effort for a feature but the technology is unfamiliar

**When NOT to Use**:
- The feasibility question is well-understood (→ just estimate and build)
- You're using spikes to avoid committing to a decision (spike procrastination)
- The "spike" keeps growing beyond the timebox (→ it's become a project, not a spike)

**Output/Deliverable**: Spike findings document: question asked, approach taken, answer found, implications for product/architecture decisions.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: for feasibility questions; directly answers "can we build this?"
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help write spike code faster, suggest technical approaches, and draft the findings document.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 45: Proof of Concept (PoC)

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A functional demonstration that a concept or approach is technically viable. More complete than a spike; focused on proving feasibility rather than user experience (in practice, the boundary with prototype can blur). Proves "this can work" before investing in full development.

**Origin**: Engineering and R&D practice, widely used in enterprise software sales and technical validation.
**Authoritative Source**: Cagan, M. (2018). *Inspired: How to Create Tech Products Customers Love*. Wiley. Also: Thomke, S. (2003). *Experimentation Matters*. Harvard Business School Press (Chapter on proof-of-concept in R&D). IEEE Standard 1012: Systems, Software, and Hardware Verification and Validation.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Feasibility
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-4 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: success criteria + scoped build + demonstration + evaluation + decision
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: engineering effort + possible third-party service usage + stakeholder demonstration effort

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define success criteria** – What must the PoC demonstrate to be considered successful? Write measurable criteria. (2-4 hours)
2. **Scope ruthlessly** – Only build what's needed to prove the concept. No UI polish, no edge cases, no scalability. (2-4 hours)
3. **Build the PoC** – Functional but not production-ready. Use shortcuts, hardcoded values, manual steps where acceptable. (1-3 weeks)
4. **Demonstrate to stakeholders** – Show the PoC working. Walk through what it proves and what it doesn't. (1-2 hours)
5. **Evaluate against criteria** – Did it meet success criteria? Document what worked, what didn't, and what remains unknown. (half day)
6. **Decide: proceed, pivot, or kill** – Based on PoC results, decide whether to invest in full development. (Decision meeting)

**When to Use**:
- A major technical decision hinges on whether an approach is viable
- Stakeholders need to see something working before approving investment
- You're evaluating a third-party technology or integration

**When NOT to Use**:
- The concept is already proven (skip to prototyping or building)
- You're using the PoC to delay a decision you could make with less effort (→ spike first)
- The PoC scope keeps expanding (it's becoming the product, not a proof)

**Output/Deliverable**: Working PoC demonstration, evaluation document with success criteria results, and go/no-go recommendation.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: directly demonstrates feasibility; weaker on desirability unless users interact with it
**AI Augmentation**: AI can accelerate PoC development by generating boilerplate code, suggesting architecture patterns, and drafting evaluation criteria.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 47: Concept Testing

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Presenting a product concept (not a working product) to potential users to gauge interest, comprehension, and perceived value. Tests the idea before building anything. Can use descriptions, storyboards, mockups, or videos.

**Origin**: Market research tradition. NN/g defines concept testing as an attitudinal method used early to determine whether users want or need a proposed product or service; applied in product discovery by Cagan and others.
**Authoritative Source**: Cagan, M. (2018). *Inspired*. Wiley. Also: Dumas, J. & Redish, J. (1999). *A Practical Guide to Usability Testing*. Intellect (Chapter on concept evaluation). Ulwick, A. (2016). *Jobs to be Done: Theory to Practice*. Idea Bite Press.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: concept creation + participant recruitment + presentation + synthesis + decision
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: concept artifact creation + participant incentives + synthesis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Create the concept artifact** – Write a product description, create a storyboard, or build a simple mockup. It must convey what the product does, for whom, and why it matters. (1-3 days)
2. **Define what you're testing** – Value (do they want it?), comprehension (do they understand it?), or differentiation (is it better than alternatives?). (1-2 hours)
3. **Recruit target users** – 5-10 people for qualitative rounds; larger samples (50+) are appropriate when concept testing is done quantitatively with structured rating questions. Match target segment. (3-5 days)
4. **Present and observe** – Show the concept. Ask: "What do you think this is? Would you use this? What would you expect to pay? What's missing?" Watch for confusion and enthusiasm. (30-45 minutes per session)
5. **Synthesize reactions** – Map responses across comprehension, desire, and concerns. Look for consistent patterns. (half day)
6. **Decide: refine, proceed, or pivot** – Based on signal strength, either iterate the concept, move to prototyping, or abandon. (Decision meeting)

**When to Use**:
- You have a product idea but want to validate interest before investing in development
- You need quick directional signal on whether an idea resonates
- You want to compare multiple concepts with the same audience

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need behavioral evidence, not opinion (→ use Fake Door or Landing Page test)
- The concept is too abstract to present meaningfully
- You're using positive reactions as definitive proof of demand (say vs. do gap)

**Output/Deliverable**: Concept test findings with comprehension scores, interest levels, quoted reactions, and go/iterate/kill recommendation.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: good directional signal; limited by say-vs-do gap – people may say they want something but not actually buy/use it
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate concept descriptions, create storyboards, and analyze reaction patterns across participants.

## METHOD 48: Pre-sales / Letter of Intent (LOI)

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Getting prospective customers to commit resources (money, time, or a written letter of intent) before the product exists. Commitment level varies—from a non-binding LOI to prepayment or a signed purchase order—with stronger commitments providing stronger demand signals.

**Origin**: Enterprise sales practice and lean startup methodology. Eric Ries and Steve Blank emphasized pre-sales as demand validation.
**Authoritative Source**: Blank, S. (2013). *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*. K&S Ranch. Also: Maurya, A. (2012). *Running Lean*. O'Reilly.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Startup, Scaleup
**Applicability Notes**: especially B2B
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: 2-8 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: narrative creation + buyer identification + sales conversations + commitment tracking + decision
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: sales effort + collateral preparation + legal review

**How to Apply**:
1. **Create a compelling product narrative** – Describe the product, its value proposition, and timeline. Be honest about what exists and what's planned. (1-2 days)
2. **Identify target buyers** – Who would buy this if it existed? Get to decision-makers, not just users. (1 week)
3. **Pitch the product** – Present to 10-20 prospects. Ask: "If we build this by [date], would you commit to [purchase/pilot/LOI]?" (2-4 weeks)
4. **Track commitment level** – Verbal interest (weak), email confirmation (moderate), signed LOI (strong), pre-payment/deposit (strongest). (Ongoing)
5. **Set a threshold** – Define what constitutes sufficient demand. Example: "5 signed LOIs from $50K+ ARR companies." (Before starting)
6. **Decide based on commitments** – Met threshold? Build. Didn't? Pivot or kill. Don't rationalize weak signals. (Decision meeting)

**When to Use**:
- You're considering a significant investment (new product, major feature, market entry)
- You need to validate willingness to pay, not just interest
- You're in B2B where pre-sales conversations are natural

**When NOT to Use**:
- B2C mass market (→ use Landing Page or Fake Door tests for demand signals)
- You can't describe the product compellingly yet (→ do Concept Testing first)
- You're using LOIs to delay building when demand is already proven

**Output/Deliverable**: Pre-sales pipeline with commitment levels, signed LOIs, and demand assessment.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real commitment from real buyers is the strongest demand signal short of actual revenue
**AI Augmentation**: AI can draft pitch decks, LOI templates, and follow-up emails. Can analyze CRM data to identify highest-probability prospects.

## METHOD 53: Kano Model Analysis

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A customer satisfaction framework that classifies features into five categories: Must-be (expected), One-dimensional (more = better), Attractive (delighters), Indifferent, and Reverse. Reveals which features drive satisfaction vs. which merely prevent dissatisfaction.

**Origin**: Noriaki Kano, Tokyo University of Science (1984). Based on the dual nature of quality – presence of desirable attributes vs. absence of undesirable ones.
**Authoritative Source**: Kano, N. et al. (1984). "Attractive quality and must-be quality." *Journal of the Japanese Society for Quality Control*. Also: Sauerwein, E. (1996). "The Kano model: How to delight your customers." *International Working Seminar on Production Economics*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: survey design + data collection + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: survey tooling + participant recruitment or incentives + analysis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Select features to evaluate** – Choose 5-15 features or concepts. Include a mix of new ideas and existing capabilities. (1-2 hours)
2. **Create Kano questionnaire** – For each feature, ask two questions: functional ("If this feature were present, how would you feel?") and dysfunctional ("If this feature were absent, how would you feel?"). 5-point scale: Like it, Expect it, Neutral, Can live with it, Dislike it. (half day)
3. **Distribute survey** – Target 50-200 respondents from your user base or target market. (1-2 weeks)
4. **Classify features** – Use the Kano evaluation table to map each functional/dysfunctional pair to a category: Must-be, One-dimensional, Attractive, Indifferent, or Reverse. (half day)
5. **Visualize and prioritize** – Plot features on the Kano diagram. Must-be features are table stakes (not differentiators). Attractive features are your opportunity for delight. (half day)
6. **Apply to roadmap** – Must-be gaps = urgent fixes. One-dimensional = competitive parity. Attractive = differentiation and delight. Indifferent = deprioritize. (Decision meeting)

**When to Use**:
- You're prioritizing a feature backlog and need to distinguish between "must have" and "nice to have"
- You want to understand which features will actually delight users vs. which are just expected
- You're in a competitive market and need to identify differentiation opportunities

**When NOT to Use**:
- You have too few respondents for meaningful analysis (30 is a rough practical starting point, not a statistical threshold—adequacy depends on number of features and variance in responses)
- Features are too abstract for users to evaluate meaningfully
- You're using it to justify not building must-be features (must-be features don't delight, but their absence creates dissatisfaction)

**Output/Deliverable**: Kano classification table, feature categorization diagram, and prioritization recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: structured customer input; limited by the hypothetical nature of the questions
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help design the Kano questionnaire, process survey responses, and generate the classification analysis.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 54: Preference Testing

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Presenting users with 2+ design or concept alternatives and asking them to choose their preference. Quick, quantitative signal on which direction resonates more. Often used for visual design, messaging, or feature framing.

**Origin**: Market research and advertising testing. Adapted for UX by tools like UsabilityHub (now Lyssna) and Maze.
**Authoritative Source**: Rubin, J. & Chisnell, D. (2008). *Handbook of Usability Testing*. Wiley. Also: Sauro, J. & Lewis, J. (2016). *Quantifying the User Experience*. Morgan Kaufmann.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: alternative creation + test setup + recruitment + result analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: testing platform + participant recruitment or incentives + analysis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Create alternatives** – 2-4 options to compare. Can be designs, layouts, copy variants, feature descriptions, or brand concepts. Keep comparable. (1-2 days)
2. **Define what you're testing** – Preference (which do you like more?), clarity (which do you understand better?), or appeal (which would you click?). (30 minutes)
3. **Set up the test** – Use a preference testing platform or a simple survey form. Show options side by side or sequentially. Ask for preference AND why. (1-2 hours)
4. **Recruit participants** – 20-50 people for directional signal, 100+ for statistical confidence. (1-2 days)
5. **Analyze results** – Count preferences. More importantly, analyze the "why" responses for patterns. (2-4 hours)
6. **Apply learnings** – Use preference data to inform the design direction. Combine with other methods for stronger evidence. (Immediate)

**When to Use**:
- You have 2+ viable options and need a quick signal on direction
- Design debates are slowing down decision-making
- You want quantitative backup for a design choice

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're testing a single option (→ use concept test or usability test)
- The options differ in functionality, not just presentation (→ run a proper A/B test)
- You're using preference as a proxy for effectiveness (users may prefer a design that performs worse)

**Output/Deliverable**: Preference test results with win rates, qualitative rationale, and design recommendation.
**Evidence Strength**: Weak-Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: quick directional signal; preference ≠ effectiveness – users may prefer what looks good over what works well
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate design variants, analyze qualitative feedback patterns, and suggest hypotheses for why one option was preferred.

## METHOD 55: Cohort Analysis

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Grouping users by a shared characteristic or time period (e.g., sign-up week, feature adoption date) and tracking their behavior over time. Reveals trends that aggregate metrics hide – like whether retention is improving for newer cohorts.

**Origin**: Statistical analysis tradition. Applied to product analytics by pioneers like Andrew Chen (Uber, a16z) and the Mixpanel/Amplitude analytics platforms.
**Authoritative Source**: Chen, A. (2017). "Cohort Analysis." In Ellis, S. & Brown, M. *Hacking Growth*. Crown Business. Also: Croll, A. & Yoskovitz, B. (2013). *Lean Analytics*. O'Reilly. McClure, D. (2007). "Startup Metrics for Pirates: AARRR!" 500 Startups (original cohort-based growth framework).

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: need sufficient user volume
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: No (requires existing usage data; historical event data is sufficient—currently live traffic is not required)
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days (setup) + ongoing monitoring
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: cohort definition + metric setup + visualization + trend review + action loop
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: analytics tooling + analysis time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the cohort** – By what attribute are you grouping users? Time-based (sign-up week) is most common. Also: acquisition channel, plan type, feature adoption date. (1-2 hours)
2. **Define the metric** – What behavior are you tracking over time? Retention, activation, revenue, feature usage. (30 minutes)
3. **Set up the analysis** – Use your analytics platform or build a spreadsheet. Row = cohort, Column = time period, Cell = metric value. (2-4 hours)
4. **Visualize** – Create a cohort table (heat map) showing how each cohort performs over time. Look for improving/declining trends across cohorts. (1-2 hours)
5. **Identify patterns** – Is retention improving for newer cohorts? Did a specific product change improve activation? Which acquisition channel produces best long-term users? (half day)
6. **Act on findings** – Use cohort insights to prioritize: double down on what's working for the best cohorts, investigate what's different for the worst. (Decision meeting)

**When to Use**:
- Aggregate metrics look flat but you suspect underlying trends (improving or declining)
- You want to measure the impact of a product change on user behavior over time
- You need to understand retention patterns beyond simple "Day 1/7/30" numbers

**When NOT to Use**:
- You don't have enough users per cohort for meaningful analysis (50 is a common practical starting point, not a universal minimum—the right threshold depends on cohort metric variance and trends you're trying to detect)
- You're looking for a one-time answer (cohort analysis is about trends over time)
- You're using cohort data without understanding why cohorts differ (→ combine with qualitative research)

**Output/Deliverable**: Cohort analysis table/chart, trend identification, and recommendations for product or marketing changes.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: behavioral data from real users over time; reveals patterns invisible in aggregate
**AI Augmentation**: AI can automate cohort segmentation, identify statistically significant differences between cohorts, and suggest hypotheses for observed patterns.

## METHOD 56: Funnel Analysis

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Mapping the sequential steps users take toward a goal (sign-up, purchase, activation) and measuring conversion/drop-off at each step. Identifies exactly where users abandon and which steps need improvement.

**Origin**: Sales funnel concept (AIDA model, 1898). Applied to product analytics by web analytics platforms and growth teams.
**Authoritative Source**: Croll, A. & Yoskovitz, B. (2013). *Lean Analytics*. O'Reilly. Also: Ellis, S. & Brown, M. (2017). *Hacking Growth*. Crown Business.

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Viability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: with sufficient user volume
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days (setup) + ongoing monitoring
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: funnel definition + instrumentation + drop-off review + diagnosis + remeasurement
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: analytics tooling + instrumentation + analysis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the funnel** – What's the critical user journey? Sign-up → Onboarding → Activation → Retention → Revenue. Be specific about each step. (1-2 hours)
2. **Instrument tracking** – Ensure each step is tracked in your analytics. (half day to 2 days)
3. **Build the funnel visualization** – Step 1 → Step 2 → Step 3, with conversion percentages between each step. (1-2 hours)
4. **Identify the biggest drop-off** – Where are you losing the most users? This is your highest-leverage optimization opportunity. (30 minutes)
5. **Diagnose the drop-off** – WHY are users dropping off at that step? Use session recordings, heatmaps, or user interviews to understand. (1-3 days)
6. **Fix and measure** – Address the drop-off cause, then re-measure the funnel. Did the conversion rate improve? (1-2 weeks)

**When to Use**:
- You have a critical user journey and need to know where users abandon
- Conversion rates are lower than expected and you need to find the bottleneck
- You want to prioritize UX improvements by impact on the overall journey

**When NOT to Use**:
- You don't have enough traffic for meaningful conversion rates (100 per step is a common practical guideline, not a statistical rule—required volume depends on expected conversion rates and the effect sizes you care about)
- The user journey isn't sequential (→ use path analysis instead)
- You're over-optimizing micro-steps when the macro problem is product-market fit

**Output/Deliverable**: Funnel visualization with conversion rates per step, identified drop-off points, and prioritized optimization recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: behavioral data showing exactly where users fall off; highly actionable
**AI Augmentation**: AI can identify unusual drop-off patterns, segment funnels by user type, and suggest optimization hypotheses based on comparable products.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 57: Feature Audit / Usage Analytics

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Systematic analysis of how existing product features are actually used – frequency, depth, user segments, and adoption curves. Separates loved features from abandoned ones. Essential for informed pruning, investment, and roadmap decisions.

**Origin**: Product analytics practice. Enabled by tools like Pendo, Amplitude, Mixpanel, and Heap.
**Authoritative Source**: Olsen, D. (2015). *The Lean Product Playbook*. Wiley. Also: Pendo (2020). *The Product Benchmarks Report*. For analytical methodology: Croll, A. & Yoskovitz, B. (2013). *Lean Analytics*. O'Reilly (Chapter 8: feature-level metrics).

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: need existing product with usage data
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: No (requires existing usage data; historical analytics are sufficient – live traffic is not required at time of analysis)
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: data collection + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: analytics tooling + analysis time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Inventory features** – List all features in your product. Group by area or module. (half day)
2. **Define usage metrics** – For each feature: DAU/MAU, frequency of use, time spent, adoption rate (% of users who use it at least once). (2-4 hours)
3. **Pull usage data** – Extract from your analytics platform. Look at minimum 30-90 days of data for reliable patterns. (1-2 days)
4. **Categorize features** – High adoption + high frequency (core), high adoption + low frequency (utility), low adoption + high frequency (niche power), low adoption + low frequency (candidates for removal). (half day)
5. **Investigate surprises** – Why is that feature never used? Is it hidden, broken, or unnecessary? Why is that niche feature used daily by a small group? (1-3 days)
6. **Make roadmap decisions** – Invest in core features. Improve discoverability of utility features. Evaluate removing low-use features. Understand niche power users. (Decision meeting)

**When to Use**:
- You're prioritizing the roadmap and want data-driven input on where to invest
- Product complexity is growing and you need to identify candidates for simplification
- You want to understand the gap between what's built and what's actually valued

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're pre-launch with no usage data (→ use concept testing, interviews)
- You only have a few features (just look at the analytics directly)
- You're using usage data alone without understanding WHY (low usage might mean the feature is hard to find, not unwanted)

**Output/Deliverable**: Feature audit report with usage data, categorization (core/utility/niche/unused), and recommendations for each category.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real behavioral data on actual usage; much stronger than stated preferences
**AI Augmentation**: AI can automate feature categorization, identify unusual usage patterns, and generate hypotheses for why certain features are underused.

## METHOD 58: Session Recording Analysis

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Watching recordings of real user sessions to observe actual behavior – clicks, scrolls, hesitations, confusion, rage clicks. Reveals how users actually interact with a product in ways that analytics alone cannot show.

**Origin**: Web analytics evolution. Tools like FullStory, Hotjar, and LogRocket made session recording accessible to product teams.
**Authoritative Source**: Albert, B. & Tullis, T. (2013). *Measuring the User Experience*. Morgan Kaufmann. For tool methodology: FullStory (2021). "Session Replay Best Practices." FullStory Resources.

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: with existing product and sufficient traffic
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: No (requires existing recorded sessions; analysis does not need live traffic at time of study)
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes (session recording captures potentially sensitive user behavior; consent and PII masking requirements vary by jurisdiction)
**Time to Execute**: 1-5 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: focus definition + filtering and setup + session review + behavior coding + prioritization
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: session recording tooling + analysis time + privacy setup

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the focus area** – Don't watch random sessions. Focus on a specific flow, page, or user segment with known issues. (30 minutes)
2. **Set up recording** – Configure your session recording tool. Filter for the target flow. Ensure PII masking is enabled. (1-2 hours)
3. **Watch 20-50 sessions** – Look for patterns: where do users pause, backtrack, click unexpectedly, or abandon? Note timestamps of interesting moments. (2-4 hours)
4. **Code behaviors** – Create a simple taxonomy: confusion (pauses, re-reads), frustration (rage clicks, rapid scrolling), abandonment (leaves mid-flow), success (completes goal). (1-2 hours)
5. **Identify patterns** – Which issues appear in 3+ sessions? These are systemic problems, not individual quirks. (1-2 hours)
6. **Prioritize fixes** – Rank by frequency × severity. Fix high-frequency, high-severity issues first. (half day)

**When to Use**:
- You know WHERE users drop off (from funnel analysis) but not WHY
- You want to see how users actually interact with a new feature post-launch
- You need to build empathy across the team for user struggles

**When NOT to Use**:
- You don't have enough traffic (< 100 sessions per flow for meaningful patterns)
- You're watching sessions without a specific hypothesis or focus (you'll waste hours)
- You're using recordings instead of talking to users (recordings show what, not why)

**Output/Deliverable**: Session analysis report with observed patterns, frequency counts, specific issues identified, and prioritized fix recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: real behavior observation; limited by lack of user intent/reasoning context
**AI Augmentation**: AI-powered session analysis tools can automatically detect rage clicks, dead clicks, and error patterns – reducing the need for manual session-by-session review.

## METHOD 59: Heatmap Analysis

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Visual representation of aggregate user behavior on a page – where they click, how far they scroll. Shows interaction patterns at a glance. Complementary to session recordings but aggregate rather than individual.

**Origin**: Web analytics practice. Popularized by tools like Crazy Egg (founded by Neil Patel, 2005) and Hotjar (2014).
**Authoritative Source**: Huang, J. et al. (2012). "No Clicks, No Problem: Using Cursor Movements to Understand and Improve Search." *Proceedings of CHI 2012*. For practitioner application: Rodden, K. & Fu, X. (2007). "Exploring how mouse movements relate to eye movements on web search results pages." *SIGIR Workshop on Web Information Seeking and Interaction*. Note: eye-tracking heatmaps (see Pernice & Nielsen 2009; Bojko 2013) are a separate method that captures actual visual attention – not click or scroll behavior.

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: with existing web product
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes (passive behavioral data collection on web pages may require consent disclosures under GDPR and similar regulations; requirements vary by jurisdiction)
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: setup + data collection + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: heatmap tooling + analysis time + privacy and consent setup

**How to Apply**:
1. **Select pages to analyze** – Focus on high-traffic pages or pages with known conversion issues. (30 minutes)
2. **Set up heatmap tracking** – Install your heatmap tool. Typically a JavaScript snippet. Configure which pages to track. (1-2 hours)
3. **Collect sufficient data** – Need sufficient pageviews for reliable patterns – commonly cited starting points are 1,000+ for click heatmaps and 2,000+ for scroll maps, but the right threshold depends on the variance in your user population and the decisions you're making. Let it run. (3-7 days)
4. **Analyze click heatmaps** – Where are users clicking? Are they clicking non-clickable elements (false affordance)? Are they missing important CTAs? (1-2 hours)
5. **Analyze scroll heatmaps** – How far do users scroll? If key content is below the fold and users don't scroll there, it's invisible. (1 hour)
6. **Apply findings** – Move important elements to high-attention zones. Fix false affordances. Improve visual hierarchy. (Design sprint or immediate fixes)

**When to Use**:
- You want a quick visual overview of how users interact with a page
- You're optimizing page layout, CTA placement, or content hierarchy
- You suspect users aren't seeing important content or are confused by the layout

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to understand user intent (heatmaps show where, not why → combine with session recordings or interviews)
- The page has very low traffic (unreliable data)
- You're analyzing complex multi-step flows (→ use funnel analysis instead)

**Time Order**: Ongoing

**How to Apply**:

## METHOD 64: Pre-mortem (Prospective Hindsight)

**Category**: Collaborative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: A structured exercise where the team imagines the product or initiative has already FAILED, then works backward to identify the most likely causes. Uses prospective hindsight (imagining a future outcome as certain) to surface risks and blind spots that forward-looking planning misses.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – Pre-mortems surface assumptions and risks by imagining failure before it happens. They generate a structured list of hypotheses worth testing, not evidence. Use it to populate an Assumption Map (M32) and prioritise which discovery methods to run next.

**Origin**: Gary Klein (1998), based on research by Deborah Mitchell, Jay Russo, and Nancy Pennington (1989) showing that prospective hindsight – imagining an event has already occurred – increases the ability to identify reasons for the outcome by approximately 30% in that specific study. The precise uplift figure is from one experimental context; the core practice is well-supported but the exact number should not be treated as a universal benchmark.
**Authoritative Source**: Klein, G. (2007). "Performing a Project Premortem." *Harvard Business Review*. Also: Klein, G. (1998). *Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions*. MIT Press. Kahneman, D. (2011). *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (endorses pre-mortem as debiasing technique).

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Feasibility, Viability, Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Small (2-5), Cross-functional (5+)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 45-90 minutes
**Time Order**: Hours
**Time Notes**: failure framing + silent brainstorm + round-robin capture + clustering + prioritization + discovery translation
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal time only

**How to Apply**:
1. **Set the scene** – "Imagine it's 12 months from now. This initiative has failed spectacularly. It's the biggest flop of the year." Make the failure vivid and certain – this is what activates prospective hindsight. (5 minutes)
2. **Silent brainstorm** – Each person writes down independently: "What caused the failure?" Give 5-10 minutes. Silence is critical – it prevents anchoring and groupthink. (10 minutes)
3. **Round-robin share** – Go around the room. Each person shares ONE reason per round. No debate, no defending, just capturing. Keep going until all unique reasons are surfaced. (15-20 minutes)
4. **Cluster and prioritize** – Group similar failure reasons. Vote on which are most likely AND most damaging. Focus on the intersection: high probability × high impact. (15 minutes)
5. **Assign mitigations** – For each top-priority failure mode: What could we do NOW to prevent it or detect it early? Assign owners and deadlines. (20 minutes)
6. **Integrate into discovery plan** – Translate the highest-risk failure modes into discovery questions. Each one becomes a risk to investigate with an appropriate method from this database. (Follow-up)

**When to Use**:
- Before making a significant investment decision (new product, major feature, pivot)
- When the team seems overconfident or has collective blind spots
- At the start of a discovery cycle to identify what risks to investigate first
- When stakeholders are pushing to skip discovery ("just build it")

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for actual discovery (pre-mortem identifies risks; you still need methods to investigate them)
- When the team culture punishes negative thinking (the exercise will be performative, not honest)
- For small, easily reversible decisions (overkill)

**Output/Deliverable**: Prioritized risk register with failure modes, likelihood assessments, mitigation actions, and mapped discovery methods for each risk.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: structured judgment, not empirical evidence; but research shows prospective hindsight significantly improves risk identification accuracy
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate additional failure scenarios from comparable products/markets, and help identify risks the team may have missed. Useful as a "10th team member" providing outside-view risks.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 65: Experience Sampling Method (ESM)

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Prompting participants at random or scheduled moments throughout their day to capture in-the-moment experiences, emotions, and behaviors. Unlike diary studies (typically participant-initiated), ESM uses researcher-triggered prompts to catch what people actually experience when it happens, reducing retrospective bias.

**Origin**: Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi and Reed Larson (1987). Originally developed to study the quality of everyday experience. Adapted for UX research and product discovery by HCI researchers at Carnegie Mellon, Microsoft Research, and others.
**Authoritative Source**: Csikszentmihalyi, M. & Larson, R. (1987). "Validity and Reliability of the Experience-Sampling Method." *Journal of Nervous and Mental Disease*. Also: Hektner, J., Schmidt, J., & Csikszentmihalyi, M. (2007). *Experience Sampling Method: Measuring the Quality of Everyday Life*. Sage.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: especially useful for products integrated into daily routines
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-4 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: sampling design + onboarding + sampling period + pattern analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: sampling tool or workflow + participant incentives + monitoring and analysis

**How to Apply**:
1. **Choose the sampling strategy** – Signal-contingent (random prompts, 5-8 per day), event-contingent (trigger after specific activity), or interval-contingent (fixed schedule, e.g., every 2 hours). Signal-contingent is most commonly used for capturing naturalistic experience; event-contingent is often better when the behavior of interest is specific and identifiable. (2-4 hours)
2. **Design the micro-survey** – 3-5 questions maximum per prompt. Mix scaled items ("How frustrated are you right now? 1-7") with open-ended ("What are you doing right now? What's on your mind?"). Must take < 2 minutes to answer. (half day)
3. **Select an ESM tool** – Dedicated ESM apps or repurpose messaging tools (SMS bots, chat platforms). The tool must support randomized timing and timestamp each response. (half day)
4. **Recruit and onboard** – 15-30 participants. Explain the protocol. Do a 1-day pilot to calibrate prompt frequency (too many = fatigue; too few = sparse data). (1 week)
5. **Run the sampling period** – 1-2 weeks. Monitor compliance rates daily. Follow up with participants who stop responding. Target 70%+ response rate. (1-2 weeks)
6. **Analyze temporal patterns** – Map experience quality across time of day, activity type, context, and emotional state. Identify when and where pain points peak, when flow states occur, and what contextual factors matter most. (2-3 days)

**When to Use**:
- You need to understand the lived experience of using your product (or the problem space) across real days and contexts
- You suspect retrospective interviews are giving you a sanitized or biased account of user experience
- Your product is used intermittently throughout the day and you need to capture real-time context

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need a quick answer (ESM takes weeks, not days → use rapid interviews instead)
- The behavior you're studying is rare or infrequent (you'll get mostly irrelevant prompts)
- Participant burden is a concern (ESM is demanding; executives, clinicians, and very busy users may not comply)
- A diary study would answer your question (if participants can reliably self-trigger reports, diary studies are simpler)

**Output/Deliverable**: Temporal experience map showing in-the-moment ratings and qualitative reports across time, context, and activity. Statistical patterns of when and where experience peaks and valleys occur.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: captures real-time experience with minimal retrospective bias; stronger ecological validity than lab-based or retrospective methods
**AI Augmentation**: AI can analyze thousands of micro-survey responses, detect temporal patterns, cluster contextual factors, and identify anomalous experience moments for researcher deep-dives.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 66: Shadowing (Passive Observation)

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Following users through their natural environment and workflow without interacting, interrupting, or asking questions. Pure observation – the researcher is a fly on the wall. Reveals unconscious behaviors, real workflows, and environmental factors that users edit out when they know they're being studied.

**Origin**: Rooted in naturalistic observation traditions in ethology (animal behavior) and sociology. Applied to design by IDEO and service design practitioners. Distinguished from Contextual Inquiry (which adds active questioning) and Ethnographic Research (which involves cultural immersion and participation).
**Authoritative Source**: McDonald, S. (2005). "Studying actions in context: a qualitative shadowing method for organizational research." *Qualitative Research*. Also: Czarniawska, B. (2007). *Shadowing: And Other Techniques for Doing Fieldwork in Modern Societies*. Copenhagen Business School Press.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: B2B contexts especially
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: access and consent + shadowing sessions + debrief + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: researcher time + travel or logistics + participant compensation

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the observation focus** – What activity, workflow, or context are you studying? Set a clear scope but stay open to surprises. (1-2 hours)
2. **Negotiate access and consent** – Explain to participants that you'll observe silently. Get written consent. Clarify that they should work normally and ignore you as much as possible. (1-3 days)
3. **Shadow silently** – Follow the participant through their workflow for 2-4 hours. No questions, no interruptions, no suggestions. Take notes on: actions, sequences, tools used, workarounds, interactions with others, environmental factors, pauses, and errors. (2-4 hours per session)
4. **Document everything** – Photograph the environment (with permission). Sketch workflows. Timestamp key moments. Note things that DON'T happen (expected steps that are skipped). (During session)
5. **Debrief AFTER** – Only after the observation period, ask clarifying questions: "I noticed you did X – can you tell me about that?" Separating observation from questioning prevents participants from self-editing their behavior to match perceived expectations (demand characteristics) and avoids contaminating observation notes with post-hoc rationalizations. (30-60 minutes)
6. **Synthesize across sessions** – Compare 3-8 shadowing sessions. What's consistent? What varies? What workarounds appear across multiple users? (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- You suspect users behave differently than they say they do (say/do gap)
- Active questioning (as in Contextual Inquiry) might change the behavior you're trying to observe
- You need to understand the full environmental context – interruptions, social dynamics, physical setup
- You're studying a workflow that is too routine for users to articulate (they can't tell you what they don't notice)

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to understand WHY users do things (shadowing shows what, not why → debrief afterward or use Contextual Inquiry)
- The environment is too private or sensitive for an observer (→ diary studies or ESM)
- Remote users make physical shadowing impractical (→ session recordings for digital behavior)
- The behavior is primarily digital with no physical component (→ session recordings are more efficient)

**Output/Deliverable**: Observation logs, workflow diagrams, environment sketches, workaround inventory, and synthesized behavioral patterns with photos.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: direct observation of real behavior without interference; stronger ecological validity than methods that involve active questioning, but limited by researcher interpretation
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help structure and analyze field notes, identify patterns across multiple shadowing sessions, and generate workflow diagrams from observation logs. Cannot replace being physically present.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 67: Sales-Led Prototype Testing (The Demo Sell)

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Presenting a working prototype to prospective customers within a real or realistic sales interaction, then measuring buying intent, objections, and deal progression. Uses the sales meeting as the test environment. Combines prototype validation with commercial signal in a single interaction.

**Origin**: B2B product-led growth and enterprise sales practices. An applied validation tactic consistent with Steve Blank's customer development work – using a real sales interaction with a working prototype instead of a pitch to test buying intent before full production investment. Not a formally standardized method.
**Authoritative Source**: Blank, S. (2013). *The Four Steps to the Epiphany*. K&S Ranch (foundational). Also: Cagan, M. (2018). *Inspired*. Wiley (on using product discovery in sales context). Weinberg, G. & Mares, J. (2015). *Traction*. Portfolio (on channel-product fit).

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: typically PM + sales + engineer
**Best For Company**: Startup, Scaleup
**Applicability Notes**: B2B especially
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: prototype prep + signal definition + sales meetings + scoring + decision
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: prototype build effort + sales-team time + meeting orchestration

**How to Apply**:
1. **Build a demo-ready prototype** – Functional enough to demonstrate the core value proposition in a sales context. Doesn't need to be production-ready, but must feel real enough that buyers can evaluate it seriously. (1-3 weeks)
2. **Define the signals** – What constitutes positive signal? Examples: "Prospect asks about pricing" (strong), "Prospect asks about implementation timeline" (strong), "Prospect says 'interesting'" (weak). Define these BEFORE the meetings. (2-4 hours)
3. **Set up real sales meetings** – Use your existing pipeline or create targeted outreach. Frame it as: "We're developing something new and would love your feedback on whether this solves your problem." (1-2 weeks)
4. **Demo as if selling** – Present the prototype as you would a real product. Watch reactions. Note where they lean in, where they check out, what questions they ask, what objections they raise. (45-60 minutes per meeting)
5. **Capture structured feedback** – After the demo, ask: "If this existed today, would you buy it? What's missing? What would you need to see to move forward? Who else would need to be involved?" (15 minutes)
6. **Score and decide** – Track signals across 5-15 meetings. If 30%+ progress to next steps (pricing discussion, follow-up, internal champion identified), the signal is strong. Below 10%? Reconsider. (Analysis meeting)

**When to Use**:
- You're in B2B and want to validate both product-market fit AND commercial viability simultaneously
- You have a working prototype and need to test it in a realistic buying context
- Pre-sales conversations alone aren't enough – buyers need to see something working
- You want to discover objections and deal-blockers before investing in production engineering

**When NOT to Use**:
- B2C (→ use landing pages, fake doors, or A/B tests for demand signals)
- You don't have a working prototype (→ do Concept Testing or Pre-sales/LOI first)
- Your sales team will oversell the prototype and set wrong expectations (prototype discipline required)
- The product isn't in a category where demos are part of the buying process

**Output/Deliverable**: Demo feedback log with buying signals, objections, feature requests, deal progression metrics, and go/no-go recommendation for production investment.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: behavioral signal strength depends on what buyers actually do – deal progression (pricing discussions, follow-up meetings, pilot requests) is strong evidence; verbal enthusiasm or positive demo reactions without forward movement is attitudinal, not behavioral, and much weaker
**AI Augmentation**: AI can analyze recorded demo sessions for engagement patterns, extract common objections, and score buying intent signals across meetings.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M29 or equivalent working prototype

## METHOD 68: Dogfooding

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Using your own product internally before releasing to external users. The team becomes the first user base, experiencing real friction, bugs, and usability issues firsthand. Turns every employee into a usability tester and every internal workflow into a test case.

**Origin**: The term "eating your own dog food" originated at Microsoft in the 1980s (attributed to Paul Maritz). Practiced extensively by Microsoft, Google, Slack, Atlassian, Apple, and most successful product companies.
**Authoritative Source**: Harrison, W. (2006). "Eating Your Own Dog Food." *IEEE Software*. Also: Internal practices documented at Microsoft (dogfooding Windows builds), Slack (using Slack internally from day one), and Google (dogfooding Chrome, Gmail, Android).

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability; secondary: Feasibility
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: ideally the whole company
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: most impactful when everyone participates; Value/Desirability signal is only valid when the internal team is a credible proxy for the target market (e.g., developer tools, collaboration tools, internal workflow products)
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: Ongoing
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: scope setup + internal deployment + real-work use + weekly synthesis + fixes
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal team time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the dogfooding scope** – Which product features or workflows are you testing internally? The entire product or a specific new feature? (1-2 hours)
2. **Set up internal use** – Deploy the product (or feature branch) for internal use. Ensure the team actually depends on it for real work, not just "tries it occasionally." (1-3 days)
3. **Establish a feedback channel** – Dedicated channel, bug report form, or weekly dogfooding standup. Make reporting frictionless. (1 hour)
4. **Use it for real work** – The key: internal use must be genuine, not performative. Use it for actual tasks, actual deadlines, actual workflows. (Ongoing, 1-4+ weeks)
5. **Catalog findings** – Track bugs, friction points, feature requests, and moments of delight. Categorize: critical (blocks work), major (workaround needed), minor (annoying), and positive (works better than expected). (Weekly synthesis)
6. **Prioritize and fix** – Use dogfooding findings as fast feedback before external release. Critical and major issues must be fixed. Minor issues inform the backlog. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- You're building a product your own team could plausibly use (tools, productivity, communication, developer tools)
- You want fast, continuous feedback without formal research recruitment
- You're about to launch a major release and need a "canary" group before general availability
- Team empathy is low – engineers and PMs don't regularly experience the product as users do

**When NOT to Use**:
- Your product is for a domain your team doesn't work in (medical software, construction tools, etc.) – your experience won't represent real users
- Internal users are too forgiving or too technically savvy to represent the target audience
- Dogfooding becomes a substitute for real user research (your team is NOT your customer)
- The product is too early-stage to use for real work (forced dogfooding produces performative feedback)

**Output/Deliverable**: Dogfooding feedback log with categorized issues, priority ranking, and release readiness assessment.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: real usage generates real bugs and usability issues, but internal users ≠ external users in expertise, patience, and context. Never use dogfooding as sole validation.
**AI Augmentation**: AI can aggregate and categorize dogfooding feedback from internal channels, identify duplicate reports, and prioritize by frequency and severity.

## METHOD 69: Conjoint Analysis

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A statistical technique that determines how users value different attributes of a product by presenting them with trade-off choices between bundles of features. Reveals the relative importance of each attribute and the optimal combination. A rigorous and widely used approach for pricing and feature-packaging decisions.

**Origin**: Mathematical psychology (Luce & Tukey, 1964). Adopted by marketing research in the 1970s-80s. Now standard in product management for pricing and packaging decisions.
**Authoritative Source**: Green, P. & Srinivasan, V. (1978). "Conjoint Analysis in Consumer Research." *Journal of Consumer Research*. Also: Orme, B. (2014). *Getting Started with Conjoint Analysis*. Research Publishers. For product context: Raghavarao, D. et al. (2010). *Choice-Based Conjoint Analysis*. CRC Press.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Viability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: requires someone with statistical literacy
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: design + fielding + analysis
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: specialist conjoint survey tooling + analyst time + participant panel

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define attributes and levels** – Choose 4-7 product attributes to test (e.g., price, features, support level, integration count). For each, define 3-4 levels (e.g., price: $29, $49, $99, $149). Keep the total manageable. (1-2 days)
2. **Choose the conjoint type** – Choice-Based Conjoint (CBC) is the most common: respondents choose between 2-3 product profiles per question. Alternative: Adaptive Conjoint (ACA) for many attributes. MaxDiff for simpler ranking questions. (half day)
3. **Design the survey** – Use conjoint software to generate choice tasks. Typically 12-20 choice tasks per respondent. Include validation tasks (dominant option that should always be chosen). (1-2 days)
4. **Field the survey** – Target 200-500 respondents from your target market for reliable results. (1-3 weeks)
5. **Analyze with statistical software** – Run Hierarchical Bayes (HB) estimation to determine part-worth utilities for each attribute level. This tells you the relative importance of each attribute and optimal combinations. (1-2 days)
6. **Build a market simulator** – Use the utility scores to simulate market share for different product configurations and pricing scenarios. "If we price at $49 with features A+B, what's our predicted share vs. competitor at $39 with features A+C?" (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- You're making pricing decisions and need to understand willingness to pay for specific features
- You need to optimize product packaging (which features in which tier)
- You're comparing multiple feature combinations and need quantitative trade-off data
- Stakeholders are debating pricing/packaging without user data

**When NOT to Use**:
- You have fewer than 150 respondents – results won't be statistically reliable (150-200 is a commonly cited practical floor; the right number depends on the number of attributes, desired precision, and whether you need segment-level estimates)
- Your product is too early-stage for users to evaluate feature trade-offs meaningfully
- The attributes you're testing aren't independent (conjoint assumes attribute independence)
- You need qualitative understanding of WHY users value certain attributes (→ combine with JTBD interviews)
- You're testing too many attributes (> 7 creates respondent fatigue and unreliable data)

**Output/Deliverable**: Part-worth utility scores, attribute importance ranking, market simulation model, and optimal product configuration recommendations with willingness-to-pay estimates.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: stated-preference method – respondents choose among hypothetical product bundles in a survey instrument, not observed behavior in a real context; forced trade-offs make it substantially stronger than direct willingness-to-pay questions, but it remains self-report data
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help design attribute/level combinations, analyze open-ended justifications from respondents, and run sensitivity analyses on the market simulator.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M47

## METHOD 70: First-Click Testing

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Measuring where users click first when given a task on a page or interface. Tests whether the information architecture and visual hierarchy guide users to the right starting point. Research shows that users who get the first click right complete tasks successfully 87% of the time vs. 46% when the first click is wrong.

**Origin**: Bob Bailey and Cari Wolfson (2006) analyzed first clicks within broader usability studies and demonstrated strong predictive validity for task completion. The standalone first-click test format – presenting a design in isolation with a single task – was later developed and popularized by tools like Optimal Workshop and UsabilityHub (now Lyssna).
**Authoritative Source**: Bailey, B. & Wolfson, C. (2006). "First Click Usability Testing." Usability.gov Research Report. Also: Sauro, J. & Lewis, J. (2016). *Quantifying the User Experience*. Morgan Kaufmann. For tool methodology: Optimal Workshop (2019). "First Click Testing Guide."

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: task design + setup + participant run + click-pattern analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: testing platform + participant incentives

**How to Apply**:
1. **Select the design to test** – Static screenshot, wireframe, or prototype of the page/screen. Can be any fidelity. (Existing asset or 1-2 hours to prepare)
2. **Write task scenarios** – 3-5 realistic tasks: "You want to change your password. Where would you click?" Tasks must have a clear correct answer. (30 minutes)
3. **Set up the test** – Upload the design to a first-click testing tool. Define click zones for correct vs. incorrect areas. (30 minutes)
4. **Recruit participants** – 20-50 participants for reliable patterns. Can use unmoderated remote testing for speed. (1-2 days)
5. **Analyze click patterns** – Generate click heatmaps. Calculate first-click success rate per task. Identify where users click incorrectly and why those areas are misleading. (1-2 hours)
6. **Iterate the design** – Fix areas with low first-click accuracy. Re-test after changes to verify improvement. (Design sprint)

**When to Use**:
- You're validating whether a page's navigation or layout guides users to the right place
- You want a quick, quantitative signal on information architecture before full usability testing
- You're comparing 2+ layout alternatives and need data on which performs better

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to test a multi-step flow (first-click only tests the starting point → use full usability testing)
- The interface has no clear "correct" first action for the task
- You're testing content comprehension rather than navigation (→ use five-second test or tree test)

**Output/Deliverable**: Click heatmap per task, first-click success rates, identification of misleading affordances, and design recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: strong predictive validity for task success; limited to initial navigation decisions only
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help generate task scenarios, analyze click distribution patterns, and identify design elements that create false affordances.

## METHOD 71: Cognitive Walkthrough

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A structured inspection method where evaluators step through a task from a new user's perspective, asking four specific questions at each step to identify learnability problems. Focuses specifically on whether first-time users can figure out how to accomplish goals without training.

**Origin**: Developed by Cathleen Wharton, John Rieman, Clayton Lewis, and Peter Polson at the University of Colorado (1994). Based on CE+ theory of exploratory learning.
**Authoritative Source**: Wharton, C. et al. (1994). "The Cognitive Walkthrough Method: A Practitioner's Guide." in Nielsen, J. & Mack, R. (eds.) *Usability Inspection Methods*. Wiley. Also: Spencer, R. (2000). "The Streamlined Cognitive Walkthrough Method." *Proceedings of CHI 2000*. Blackmon, M. et al. (2002). "Cognitive Walkthrough for the Web." *Proceedings of CHI 2002*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-8 hours per task flow
**Time Order**: Hours
**Time Notes**: user-profile definition + action-sequence mapping + walkthrough + failure analysis + recommendations
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal evaluator time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the user profile** – Who is the target user? What is their goal? What experience/knowledge do they bring? Write this down explicitly – the walkthrough is from THIS user's perspective. (30 minutes)
2. **Define the task and correct action sequence** – List every step the user must take to complete the task. Be granular: "Click the Settings icon → Select Account → Click Change Password → Enter old password → Enter new password → Click Save." (30-60 minutes)
3. **Walk through each step, asking four questions** – At EACH action in the sequence, ask:
   - Will the user try to achieve the right effect? (Do they know what to do next?)
   - Will the user notice the correct action is available? (Is the control visible?)
   - Will the user associate the correct action with the desired effect? (Is the label/icon clear?)
   - If the user performs the correct action, will they see progress? (Is there feedback?)
   Record a success/failure for each question at each step. (1-4 hours)
4. **Document failure points** – Where a question gets a "no" answer, document: the step, which question failed, why, and the likely user behavior (confusion, wrong path, abandonment). (During walkthrough)
5. **Prioritize by severity** – Failures at early steps are more critical (users may never reach later steps). Failures on question 1 (motivation) are harder to fix than question 2 (visibility). (30 minutes)
6. **Recommend fixes** – For each failure: what design change would turn the "no" into a "yes"? Be specific. (1-2 hours)

**When to Use**:
- You want to evaluate learnability for new/first-time users without recruiting participants
- You're designing onboarding flows, setup wizards, or first-run experiences
- You need a learnability-focused inspection method – cognitive walkthrough examines a different dimension of usability than a general heuristic review (learnability vs. broad principles)
- Budget or time prevents formal usability testing (cognitive walkthrough is faster and cheaper)

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're evaluating expert/power user workflows (cognitive walkthrough is about learnability, not efficiency)
- You want to understand real user behavior (this is expert inspection, not user testing → use usability testing)
- The interface is exploratory with no single correct path (the method requires a defined action sequence)

**Output/Deliverable**: Step-by-step walkthrough document with success/failure ratings per question, identified learnability barriers, severity rankings, and specific design recommendations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: structured expert judgment; catches genuine learnability issues but may miss problems that only real users encounter. Best combined with actual usability testing.
**AI Augmentation**: AI can simulate the walkthrough by analyzing UI screenshots against the four questions, identify likely confusion points, and draft the walkthrough document for expert review and validation.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 72: Beta Testing / Controlled Pilot

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Core
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Releasing a near-complete product or feature to a limited group of real external users under controlled conditions before general availability. Collects real-world usage data, bugs, and feedback at scale that internal testing and lab studies cannot replicate. Beta testing is typically a product-preview release; a controlled pilot is a more operationally focused field deployment – related but distinct formats.

**Origin**: Software development practice dating to IBM's 1950s-60s terminology (alpha = internal testing, beta = external testing). Formalized as a product validation practice across all software categories.
**Authoritative Source**: Dolan, R. & Matthews, J. (1993). "Maximizing the Utility of Customer Product Testing: Beta Test Design and Management." *Journal of Product Innovation Management*. Also: Cagan, M. (2018). *Inspired*. Wiley (Chapter on beta programs). For modern practice: Microsoft (2014). "Windows Insider Program" documentation (scaled beta model).

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability, Feasibility
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No (beta programs often recruit prospective or non-customer users; existing users not required)
**Needs Live Traffic**: No (controlled beta operates under restricted access; general public traffic is not required)
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-8 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: objective definition + participant selection + instrumentation + beta run + analysis
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: infrastructure provisioning + support overhead + participant management + incentives

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define beta objectives** – What specific questions must the beta answer? Feature completeness? Scalability? Usability in real environments? Market fit? Write measurable success criteria. (half day)
2. **Select beta participants** – Choose 20-500 users representing target segments. Mix of enthusiasts and mainstream users. For B2B: select accounts that match your ICP. Require a beta agreement covering feedback obligations and NDA. (1-2 weeks)
3. **Instrument for data collection** – Set up analytics, error tracking, feedback mechanisms, and support channels BEFORE launch. You must be able to measure what matters. (1 week)
4. **Launch and support** – Release to beta group. Provide dedicated support channel. Set expectations: feedback cadence, known limitations, how to report issues. (Launch day)
5. **Monitor and iterate** – Track usage metrics, bug reports, feature requests, and satisfaction scores throughout. Fix critical issues in real-time. Run mid-beta check-ins with participants. (Throughout beta period)
6. **Analyze and decide** – Did you hit success criteria? What's the quality/readiness signal? Categorize findings: must-fix-before-launch, should-fix-soon, nice-to-have. Make the GA decision. (1 week post-beta)

**When to Use**:
- You need real-world validation that lab testing and dogfooding can't provide (diverse environments, real data, real workflows)
- You're launching a major release and need to de-risk before general availability
- You want early adopters to become advocates and provide social proof at launch
- The product involves integrations, infrastructure, or enterprise contexts that can only be tested in the field

**When NOT to Use**:
- You're using beta as a crutch to avoid making the product ready (perpetual beta anti-pattern)
- A smaller, faster experiment would answer your question (→ fake door, usability test, dogfooding)
- You can't properly support beta users (frustrated beta testers become detractors, not advocates)
- The beta lacks clear success criteria (you'll get feedback but not answers)

**Output/Deliverable**: Beta analysis report with usage metrics, bug severity distribution, satisfaction scores, feature feedback themes, and GA readiness recommendation.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: real users, real environments, real workflows at meaningful scale; the closest to production truth you can get before launch
**AI Augmentation**: AI can triage and categorize beta feedback at scale, detect sentiment patterns, identify the most impactful bugs by user-impact analysis, and generate beta summary reports.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M45

## METHOD 73: Usability Benchmarking (Standardized Scoring)

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Measuring perceived usability with a validated, standardized instrument – primarily SUS (System Usability Scale) or UMUX-Lite – to produce a comparable score that can be tracked over time, benchmarked against industry norms, and compared across product versions or competitors. Distinct from qualitative usability testing: the goal is a reliable score, not problem discovery.

**Origin**: SUS (System Usability Scale) created by John Brooke at Digital Equipment Corporation (1986), published in Usability Evaluation in Industry (1996). UMUX-Lite (Usability Metric for User Experience) developed by Kraig Finstad (2010) as a shorter, copyright-free alternative. Both are psychometrically validated instruments.
**Authoritative Source**: Brooke, J. (1996). SUS: A 'quick and dirty' usability scale. In P. Jordan et al. (Eds.), *Usability Evaluation in Industry*. Taylor & Francis. Also: Finstad, K. (2010). The usability metric for user experience. *Interacting with Computers*, 22(5), 323-327. Bangor, A., Kortum, P., & Miller, J. (2009). Determining what individual SUS scores mean. *Journal of Usability Studies*, 4(3), 114-123 – provides the A-F grading scale and industry benchmarks.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: survey design + distribution + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: participant incentives + lightweight survey tooling

**How to Apply**:
1. **Select instrument** – SUS (10 items, widely benchmarked) or UMUX-Lite (2 items, faster). SUS is preferred when industry benchmarking is the goal; UMUX-Lite when survey length is a constraint. (30 minutes)
2. **Define the evaluation scope** – Specify which product, feature, or flow participants will use before responding. A SUS score for "the whole product" is less actionable than a score for "the onboarding flow." (1-2 hours)
3. **Recruit participants** – 8-10 is a commonly cited practical floor for a reliable score; 20+ is often recommended for greater stability. The right number depends on whether you need a confidence interval, a version comparison, or a segment-level breakdown – larger samples are needed for narrower intervals or subgroup analysis. Recruit actual users of the specified scope, not general participants. (1-5 days)
4. **Administer the survey** – Present immediately after a task or product interaction, before the experience fades. Do not coach or assist; SUS measures impressions, not performance. (Minutes per participant)
5. **Calculate scores** – SUS scoring: odd items (1,3,5,7,9): score – 1; even items (2,4,6,8,10): 5 – score; sum all 10 values × 2.5 = score 0-100. UMUX-Lite has its own scoring formula. (1 hour)
6. **Interpret and benchmark** – The Bangor et al. (2009) grading convention is widely used: 85+ = Excellent (A), 68-84 = OK (B-C), below 68 = below average (needs attention), below 51 = Failing (F). Treat these as practical benchmarking conventions, not universal thresholds – what constitutes "acceptable" varies by product category, user population, and task complexity. Compare primarily to your own previous scores and category-specific benchmarks where available. (1-2 hours)
7. **Pair with qualitative follow-up** – A score tells you where you are; interviews or usability testing tell you why. Schedule targeted M16 sessions for any score below 68. (As needed)

**When to Use**:
- Tracking usability improvement across release cycles with a consistent, comparable metric
- Comparing the redesign against the current version on a common scale
- Needing a single defensible usability metric for stakeholder reporting or competitive benchmarking
- As a lightweight follow-up signal after moderated usability testing to track whether fixes worked

**When NOT to Use**:
- You need to find specific usability problems (SUS scores don't tell you what to fix – use M16 or M17)
- The product is too new for users to have formed a usability impression
- Using a single rising score as the only usability signal without understanding the problems underneath it
- Benchmarking against a non-comparable population (different user type, different task scope)

**Output/Deliverable**: SUS or UMUX-Lite score(s) with trend over time, benchmark comparison, and grade interpretation. Optional: score by segment or by flow.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: psychometrically validated instrument; reliable for tracking and comparison; does not diagnose root causes
**AI Augmentation**: Process SUS/UMUX-Lite responses at scale, calculate scores automatically, flag statistically significant changes across versions, and generate benchmark comparison reports. Cannot determine why a score changed – benchmarking measures score movement; qualitative testing (M16/M17) explains the cause.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 74: In-Product Intercept Research

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Triggering a brief research interaction – a micro-survey, short feedback prompt, or interview recruitment invitation – at a specific moment in the user's actual product experience. Captures contextual signal when the experience is fresh, without requiring separate recruitment or lab settings.

**Origin**: Grew from the expansion of in-product survey and NPS tooling in the 2010s – tools like Sprig (formerly UserLeap), Pendo, and Hotjar made event-triggered in-context prompts accessible to product teams. Referenced as a continuous discovery practice by Torres (*Continuous Discovery Habits*, 2021). Distinct from NPS (M12) and general surveys (M11) in its in-context, moment-specific triggering.
**Authoritative Source**: Torres, T. (2021). *Continuous Discovery Habits*. Product Talk LLC. For implementation: Hotjar, Sprig (formerly UserLeap), and Pendo documentation on in-product research design. Also: Sauro, J. & Lewis, J.R. (2016). *Quantifying the User Experience* – chapter on in-situ measurement.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: requires live product with sufficient active users
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: Days to 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: setup + collection + analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: intercept tooling + optional incentives

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the research moment** – Identify the specific in-product event that should trigger the intercept: task completion, feature first use, drop-off, session duration milestone. The trigger must be meaningful – something that indicates the user has just had a relevant experience. (1-2 hours)
2. **Write the research question** – One primary question with a clear decision tied to it. "After onboarding completion, do users understand what to do next?" If you can't state the decision the answer will inform, don't run the intercept. (1-2 hours)
3. **Design the intercept** – Micro-survey (2-4 questions max), sentiment emoji, open text, or recruitment invitation for a follow-up interview. Match format to the moment: low-effort prompts for mid-task; richer questions immediately post-task. (2-4 hours)
4. **Configure targeting and frequency** – Set the trigger event, sampling rate (not every user, every time), suppression rules (don't ask the same user more than once per N days), and exclusion criteria (support tickets open, trial expiring). (2-4 hours)
5. **Run and monitor** – Watch response rates daily. Below 10% means the trigger moment is wrong, the question is unclear, or intercept fatigue has set in. (Throughout collection)
6. **Analyze and act** – Cluster open-text responses, calculate satisfaction/clarity metrics, identify users who should be recruited for deeper interviews (M1). Close the loop: what decision did this inform? (1-3 days)

**When to Use**:
- Immediately after a key interaction (onboarding, first feature use, checkout, support resolution) where contextual accuracy is highest
- Recruiting follow-up interview participants from a relevant in-context moment
- Measuring satisfaction or clarity at a specific touchpoint rather than overall
- When email survey response rates are too low to be useful for specific-moment questions

**When NOT to Use**:
- Interrupting users during complex or high-stakes tasks – this damages trust and produces frustrated responses
- Overusing intercepts until users dismiss them habitually (response rate decline is the warning signal)
- Asking questions answerable by behavioral analytics – don't ask "did you complete the task?" when your funnel data already answers it
- Products without enough active users to generate meaningful signal in a reasonable time window

**Output/Deliverable**: Quantitative summary of responses (satisfaction scores, yes/no rates), qualitative themes from open-text, recruited participants for follow-up interviews, and a decision update tied to the original research question.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: high contextual accuracy but self-selected response, moment-specific not holistic
**AI Augmentation**: Analyze large volumes of intercept responses, detect sentiment patterns across touchpoints, cluster qualitative feedback by feature or journey stage, and flag touchpoints with unusually low satisfaction for follow-up. Cannot replace the contextual interpretation of why a user felt frustrated or delighted at a specific moment – that requires interview follow-up (M1).
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 75: Data-flow Mapping

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Systematic mapping of what personal data a product collects, why it is collected, where it moves, who can access it, how long it is retained, and when it is deleted. Produces a data inventory and flow map – a practical and widely-used approach for meeting GDPR documentation obligations, and a standard input to DPIA, LIA, and Article 30 records of processing activities (RoPA).

**Origin**: Rooted in data governance and information security practice (data flow diagrams, DFDs, developed by DeMarco and Yourdon in the 1970s). Adopted as a privacy-specific practice to support compliance with GDPR (2018) and equivalent frameworks – GDPR does not mandate "data-flow mapping" by name, but requires organisations to document their processing activities (Article 30 RoPA) and conduct DPIAs where needed; data-flow mapping is a practical and widely-recognised method for meeting those obligations. The ICO explicitly identifies data-flow mapping as a practical starting point for documenting processing activities.
**Authoritative Source**: ICO (UK Information Commissioner's Office). *Data mapping: how to document your personal data processing*. ico.org.uk. Also: EDPB (European Data Protection Board). *Guidelines 01/2020 on processing personal data in the context of connected vehicles*. For practical methodology: Cavoukian, A. (2011). *Privacy by Design: The 7 Foundational Principles*. Information and Privacy Commissioner of Ontario.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: engineering, legal/privacy, product, data
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: Days to 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: scope definition + data inventory + flow tracing + retention review + legal/engineering validation
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: cross-functional workshop time + documentation effort + optional inventory tooling

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define scope** – Which product, service, or feature are you mapping? Narrow to a specific data processing activity or system boundary. (1-2 hours)
2. **Identify all data inputs** – List every piece of personal data the system collects: what it is, from whom, through what mechanism (form, API, tracking, inference). (Half day)
3. **Trace data flows** – For each data type, document: where it is stored, who can access it (internal roles, third-party processors, sub-processors), and what it is used for. Draw the flow. (1-2 days)
4. **Document retention and deletion** – For each data type: how long is it retained? Is there an automated deletion process? What triggers deletion? (Half day)
5. **Identify legal basis** – For each processing activity, note the GDPR lawful basis (or equivalent). Flag activities without a clear basis for follow-up. (Half day, with legal input)
6. **Review and validate** – Walk through the map with engineering and legal to identify gaps, undocumented flows (shadow IT, analytics third parties), and discrepancies. (Half day)
7. **Document and maintain** – Record the map in a format that can be updated as the system changes. Assign an owner. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Before a DPIA or LIA – the data map is the required input
- When building a new feature that touches personal data
- When entering a new jurisdiction or onboarding a new third-party data processor
- When the team cannot answer "what data do we hold and where does it go?"

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a one-time artifact that is never updated – an outdated data map creates false confidence
- As a substitute for a DPIA when a DPIA is legally required
- For products that collect no personal data

**Output/Deliverable**: Data inventory and flow diagram showing all personal data types, their sources, storage locations, access controls, purposes, and retention rules. Often feeds directly into Article 30 RoPA and DPIA inputs.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: structured factual inventory; if accurate, provides a defensible foundation for compliance assessments
**AI Augmentation**: AI can parse data schemas, API documentation, and code repositories to generate a draft data inventory for human verification. Cannot determine lawful basis, processing purposes, or retention obligations – those require human legal and product input.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 76: Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA / PIA)

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Structured assessment of whether planned personal data processing is likely to result in high risk to the rights and freedoms of individuals, what those risks are, and what mitigations are needed before launch. Under GDPR Article 35 a DPIA is legally mandatory before certain high-risk processing begins. A Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) is a conceptually similar practice used outside the EU – less standardised across jurisdictions but widely adopted under US, Canadian, and Australian privacy frameworks.

**Origin**: The PIA concept originated in US federal government practice (E-Government Act of 2002, OMB Circular A-130). The GDPR DPIA (2018) formalised and mandated a structured version for EU/UK contexts. The EDPB (formerly WP29) has published detailed guidelines on DPIAs. The ICO publishes the most widely used practitioner guidance in English.
**Authoritative Source**: GDPR, Article 35 (Regulation (EU) 2016/679). EDPB (WP29). *Guidelines on Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA)*, WP 248 rev.01. ICO. *Data protection impact assessments*. ico.org.uk. For PIA methodology: IAPP (International Association of Privacy Professionals). *Privacy Impact Assessment*. iapp.org.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: product, legal/privacy, engineering
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: trigger screening + processing description + necessity/proportionality review + risk assessment + mitigation review + documentation
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: cross-functional assessment time + legal/privacy review + possible outside counsel

**How to Apply**:
1. **Screen for mandatory triggers** – Check GDPR Article 35(3) and your supervisory authority's published mandatory list. The general rule is that a DPIA is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to individuals' rights and freedoms. Common indicators and examples include: systematic profiling with legal or similarly significant effects, large-scale processing of special category data, systematic surveillance of public areas, biometric or genetic data for identification purposes, and automated decision-making. This is not a closed checklist – EDPB guidance (WP248) and national supervisory authority lists provide additional criteria. If in doubt, consult your DPO or legal counsel. (1-2 hours, with legal input)
2. **Describe the processing** – Document what data is processed, for what purpose, by whom, how long it is retained, and who has access. Use the data-flow map (M75) as input. (Half day)
3. **Assess necessity and proportionality** – Is the processing limited to what is necessary for the stated purpose? Is the purpose legitimate? Could a less privacy-invasive approach achieve the same goal? (Half day, with legal and product)
4. **Identify and assess risks** – For each processing activity, identify risks to individuals' rights and freedoms: likelihood and severity. Consider both internal failure risks (breach, misuse) and structural risks (the processing itself causes harm). (1 day)
5. **Identify mitigations** – For each identified risk, document the technical and organisational measures that reduce it. Assess residual risk after mitigations. (Half day)
6. **Consult the DPO** – Where a DPO exists, they must be consulted (Article 35(2)). If residual risk remains high, prior consultation with the supervisory authority is required (Article 36). (As needed)
7. **Document and review** – Record the DPIA, link it to the product feature, and schedule a review date. The DPIA must be revisited if the processing changes materially. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Before launching a feature meeting GDPR Article 35 mandatory triggers
- As a structured risk-discovery exercise for any new product handling personal data at scale, even below the mandatory threshold
- Before deploying a high-risk AI system that processes personal data (complement with M83 FRIA)
- When entering a new EU market or processing EU resident data in new ways

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a retrospective checkbox after launch – must be completed before processing begins
- As a substitute for FRIA (M83) when the EU AI Act requires one – run both
- When processing clearly falls below mandatory thresholds (a lighter data-flow map and screening may suffice)

**Output/Deliverable**: DPIA report documenting processing description, necessity and proportionality assessment, identified risks, mitigations, residual risk, and DPO/supervisory authority consultation record where required.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Legal Assessment
**Evidence Notes**: standards-based, GDPR-mandated structure; legally defensible if conducted properly
**AI Augmentation**: AI can populate a DPIA template from data-flow map and feature specification, and flag processing activities against mandatory-trigger criteria. Cannot assess necessity and proportionality, determine actual risk to individuals, or substitute for qualified legal review.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M75

## METHOD 77: Privacy Threat Modeling (LINDDUN)

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Structured identification of privacy threats in a system's architecture, data flows, interfaces, and integrations before release. LINDDUN (KU Leuven; linddun.org) is the leading privacy-specific threat-modeling framework, covering seven canonical threat categories: Linking, Identifying, Non-repudiation, Detecting, Data Disclosure, Unawareness, and Non-compliance. Applies at design stage before code is committed. Distinct from STRIDE, which is a security threat model and does not address privacy-specific threats.

**Origin**: LINDDUN developed by Sabrina Capitanio, Koen Yskout, Dimitri Van Landuyt, Wouter Joosen, and Kim Wuyts at KU Leuven (Belgium), first published 2010. The framework has been refined through ongoing academic and practitioner work at KU Leuven's DistriNet research group. LINDDUN GO (a lightweight card-based variant) published 2020.
**Authoritative Source**: Wuyts, K. et al. (2015). "LINDDUN Privacy Threat Modeling: A Tutorial." *Technical Report CW 685*, KU Leuven. Wuyts, K. & Joosen, W. (2015). "LINDDUN Privacy Threat Modeling." KU Leuven DistriNet. Official site and methodology: linddun.org. For the lightweight variant: Wuyts, K. et al. (2020). "LINDDUN GO: A Lightweight Approach to Privacy Threat Modeling." *IEEE SecDev 2020*.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Space Notes**: primarily applied at design/architecture stage; also used at Validate stage on near-final system designs
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: privacy, security, engineering, product
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: requires defined architecture
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: Days to 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: DFD creation + threat walkthrough + prioritization + mitigation mapping + DPIA handoff
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: cross-functional workshop time + facilitation + design/engineering follow-up

**How to Apply**:
1. **Create a Data Flow Diagram (DFD)** – Map all system entities (external, internal), processes, data stores, and data flows. Use the output of M75 (Data-flow Mapping). Level 0 DFD is sufficient to start. (Half day)
2. **Select the LINDDUN variant** – Full LINDDUN for complex systems; LINDDUN GO (card-based) for lighter-weight workshops; LINDDUN Privacy Patterns for design-level guidance. (1 hour)
3. **Apply the threat categories per DFD element** – For each DFD element (data flow, process, data store, external entity), systematically ask whether each LINDDUN threat applies: Linking (can data be combined to infer more?), Identifying (can individuals be identified?), Non-repudiation (can actions be denied?), Detecting (can individual presence be inferred?), Data Disclosure (can data be accessed without authorisation?), Unawareness (are users unaware of processing?), Non-compliance (does processing violate regulations?). (1-3 days)
4. **Document threats** – For each identified threat: describe the threat scenario, affected data types, affected individuals, and potential impact. Use the LINDDUN threat tree templates. (1 day)
5. **Prioritise threats** – Rate by likelihood and impact. Focus remediation effort on high-priority threats. (Half day)
6. **Identify mitigations** – Map each threat to applicable privacy-enhancing technologies (PETs) or design changes. LINDDUN Privacy Patterns provide a structured mitigation catalogue. (1 day)
7. **Feed into DPIA** – Use the threat model output as structured input to the DPIA risk section (M76). The threat model identifies what could go wrong; the DPIA assesses whether the risk is acceptable. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- After data-flow mapping, once architecture is sufficiently defined
- Before a DPIA, to identify specific threat scenarios the assessment must address
- For any product involving AI, automated decisions, or third-party data sharing where privacy risk is non-obvious
- When your security team has run STRIDE and you need to complement it with privacy-specific analysis

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for a DPIA – threat modeling identifies threats; the DPIA assesses risk and required mitigations
- Before architecture is defined (no DFD = no meaningful threat analysis)
- As a solo legal exercise – requires engineering understanding of system behavior

**Output/Deliverable**: Privacy threat model: DFD with annotated threats, prioritised threat list, threat-mitigation mapping, and recommended design changes or PETs. Feeds into DPIA risk section.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: structured, systematic coverage of privacy threat space; quality depends on DFD accuracy and team expertise
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate an initial list of LINDDUN threat scenarios from an architecture description or DFD for the team to validate and extend. Cannot assess which threats are realistic in context or replace engineering judgment about actual system behavior.

## METHOD 78: Participatory Engagement with Affected Communities

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Operating Practice
**Short Description**: Structured research engagement with people who may be affected by the product – including non-users, communities, civil-society representatives, domain experts, and people with lived experience of relevant harms or exclusions. Used to surface rights impacts, discrimination risks, and unintended consequences that internal teams consistently miss. Recommended by NIST AI RMF as a structured way to gather input from affected communities and civil society when building AI systems. Distinct from standard user interviews (M1): the purpose is harm and rights discovery, not value or usability validation.

**Origin**: Rooted in participatory design research (Scandinavian tradition, 1970s-80s; Bjerknes & Bratteteig, Nygaard). Extended into AI ethics and human rights practice by organisations including the Partnership on AI, AI Now Institute, Algorithmic Justice League, and NIST. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF, 2023) explicitly includes participatory engagement as a structured socio-technical practice for AI risk identification.
**Authoritative Source**: NIST. (2023). *AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)*. NIST AI 100-1. Section: Socio-technical approaches to risk identification. Also: Arnstein, S. (1969). "A Ladder of Citizen Participation." *Journal of the American Institute of Planners*. For AI-specific practice: Partnership on AI. (2021). *About ML: Annotation and Benchmarking on Understanding and Evaluating AI for Real-World Impact*. Costanza-Chock, S. (2020). *Design Justice*. MIT Press.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Space Notes**: Problem Space (primarily), also Solution Space for Validate-stage harm testing
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: requires community engagement or research ethics experience
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: most critical for AI products, public-impact services, and products affecting vulnerable populations
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 weeks minimum
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: community identification + participation design + recruitment + engagement sessions + harm synthesis + response loop
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: community recruitment + fair compensation + facilitation + trust-building logistics

**How to Apply**:
1. **Identify affected communities** – Who beyond the direct user could be affected? Consider: marginalised groups, non-users who interact with the system indirectly, communities in specific geographic or regulatory contexts, people with disabilities, children. Do not assume your current user base is representative. (Half day)
2. **Design for genuine participation** – Define the engagement format: interviews, focus groups, co-design workshops, surveys, community advisory boards. Choose the format appropriate to the community and the question. Ensure participation is compensated fairly. (1-2 days)
3. **Recruit with care** – Do not recruit through your existing product user channels alone. Work with community organisations, civil-society groups, or specialist recruitment partners to reach people outside your current base. (1-2 weeks)
4. **Run the engagement** – Focus on: what harms could this product cause? What exclusions? What rights does it affect? What would make you trust or distrust it? What would you want changed? Use structured prompts but allow open exploration. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Synthesise harm and risk findings** – Cluster findings by harm type and affected group. Identify patterns that appear across multiple participants or communities. Flag surprises – they represent your most important blind spots. (2-3 days)
6. **Feed findings into product decisions** – Map each identified harm or risk to a product design question. Create a documented response for each: what will change, what is a known residual risk, what needs further investigation. (1 day)
7. **Close the loop** – Share findings and product responses back with participating communities where possible. This is both ethical practice and improves future engagement quality. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- Building products with consequential decisions affecting people – employment tools, credit, healthcare, education, public services, or AI systems
- When DPIA or FRIA (M83) identifies impact on vulnerable or marginalised groups
- Before designing consent or transparency mechanisms for populations with low digital literacy
- When your team lacks lived experience of the affected context

**When NOT to Use**:
- As PR rather than genuine input into product decisions
- When participation is not compensated (extractive research damages community trust and produces poor data)
- For low-risk features with no meaningful impact on rights or wellbeing
- As a substitute for other compliance assessments – this generates qualitative insight, not legal sign-off

**Output/Deliverable**: Harm and risk findings report: identified harm types, affected groups, representative quotes, risk prioritisation, and recommended product changes. Often includes a documented response to each finding.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: rich qualitative insight from directly affected people; not statistically generalisable but captures harm scenarios that quantitative methods miss entirely
**AI Augmentation**: AI can synthesise themes across session notes, support multilingual note translation, and structure harm scenarios from raw findings. Cannot replace genuine engagement – AI-simulated community voices are not a substitute for real affected people and produce systematically biased outputs.

## METHOD 79: Transparency and Notice Comprehension Testing

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: User testing of privacy notices, consent language, cookie banners, and AI interaction disclosures – to determine whether people can find, read, understand, and act on them. Tests comprehension and usability, not legal presence alone. The ICO explicitly recommends user-testing privacy information before launch to check whether people can access and understand it.

**Origin**: Rooted in plain-language research (Redish, J.C., 1985 onwards) and usability testing methodology. Applied specifically to privacy notices as a compliance practice from the early GDPR implementation period (2018-2020). The ICO, EDPB, and FTC have all published guidance emphasising that legal presence of a notice does not satisfy transparency obligations if users cannot understand it.
**Authoritative Source**: ICO. *Write your privacy information clearly*. ico.org.uk (guidance on plain language and user testing for privacy notices). EDPB. *Guidelines 05/2020 on Consent under Regulation 2016/679*. For methodology: Redish, J.C. (2012). *Letting Go of the Words: Writing Web Content that Works*. Morgan Kaufmann. Nielsen, J. (1993). *Usability Engineering*. Morgan Kaufmann (usability testing methodology).

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: Days to 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: notice selection + comprehension-question design + testing sessions + rewrite + re-test + documentation
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: participant incentives + researcher/moderation time + rewrite iteration

**How to Apply**:
1. **Identify what to test** – Privacy notice? Cookie banner? Consent flow? AI interaction disclosure? Data subject rights information? Select the specific notice or flow to test. (1-2 hours)
2. **Define comprehension questions** – What do you need users to understand? Examples: What data is collected? What are their rights? What does this AI feature do with their data? How do they opt out? Draft 5-8 specific comprehension questions. (2-3 hours)
3. **Recruit representative participants** – Target your actual user population. If the notice covers vulnerable users or users with low digital literacy, include them specifically. 5-8 participants is usually sufficient. (1-2 days)
4. **Run think-aloud sessions** – Show participants the notice or consent flow. Ask them to read it and think aloud. Then ask your comprehension questions without prompting. Observe: where do they struggle, what do they misunderstand, what do they skip? (30-45 min per participant)
5. **Measure comprehension** – Score each participant on each comprehension question. A common threshold: 80% of participants should correctly answer each key question. Identify which questions fail this threshold. (After each session)
6. **Revise and re-test** – Rewrite sections that fail comprehension checks. Use plain language: short sentences, common words, active voice. Re-test with a new set of participants if changes are substantial. (1-3 days)
7. **Document results** – Record comprehension scores, specific failure points, and changes made. This documentation supports a legal position that genuine efforts were made to ensure transparency. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Before launching a new privacy notice, consent flow, or cookie banner
- When redesigning disclosures after a regulatory change (GDPR, AI Act, CCPA)
- Before deploying an AI feature with a required user-facing disclosure
- When legal has written the notice and no one has verified that real users can understand it

**When NOT to Use**:
- As legal sign-off – comprehension testing confirms understanding, not legal compliance
- As a substitute for DPIA
- When the product has no user-facing privacy or transparency mechanisms to test

**Output/Deliverable**: Comprehension test report with per-question pass/fail rates, identified failure points, participant quotes, and revised notice text. Provides evidence of genuine transparency effort.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Observational
**Evidence Notes**: evidence comes from observing where participants struggle, skip, or misunderstand during think-aloud sessions, combined with verbal comprehension responses – a mix of observed task behavior and self-report; stronger than readability scores alone but small sample limits statistical confidence
**AI Augmentation**: AI can analyse notice text for reading complexity (Flesch-Kincaid, SMOG), flag legal jargon likely to cause comprehension failure, and generate plain-language rewrite drafts for legal review. Cannot replace testing with real users – automated scores do not predict whether users understand specific consent trade-offs or their rights.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M76

## METHOD 80: Fairness and Bias Assessment

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Evaluation of whether a system performs differently across demographic groups or creates unequal harm – including disaggregated performance analysis and subgroup testing for disparate impact. The primary validation method for discrimination and equity risk in AI and algorithmic products. EU AI Act obligations for high-risk AI systems (Articles 9 and 10) require training and testing data to be sufficiently representative, bias to be minimised, and bias detection and correction to be supported. Mandated as an independent third-party audit in specific jurisdictions for specific use cases (e.g. NYC Local Law 144 for AI-assisted employment decisions in New York City).

**Origin**: Rooted in anti-discrimination law and statistical disparate impact analysis (US Civil Rights Act, 1964; Griggs v. Duke Power, 1971). Operationalised as a technical product method through fairness-aware machine learning research (Dwork et al., 2012; Hardt, Price & Srebo, 2016). Mandated as a product practice by NYC Local Law 144 (2021) and EU AI Act (2024).
**Authoritative Source**: Hardt, M., Price, E. & Srebro, N. (2016). "Equality of Opportunity in Supervised Learning." *NeurIPS 2016*. Also: Barocas, S., Hardt, M. & Narayanan, A. (2023). *Fairness and Machine Learning: Limitations and Opportunities*. MIT Press (fairmlbook.org – freely available). For legal context: NYC Local Law 144 (2021). EU AI Act, Articles 9 and 10, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: data science, product, legal; external review for high-stakes systems
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: fairness-question definition + disaggregated analysis + disparity assessment + mitigation + re-evaluation + documentation
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: internal data science/legal time + possible external audit support

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the fairness question** – What decisions or outputs might disadvantage certain groups? Which demographic attributes are relevant (race, gender, age, disability, postcode as a proxy)? What outcome disparity would constitute harm? Define this before running analysis to avoid post-hoc rationalisation. (Half day, with legal and product)
2. **Collect disaggregated data** – Obtain ground-truth labels, system outputs, and demographic data for a representative evaluation dataset. If demographic data is unavailable, investigate proxy-based methods or data collection improvements. (1-3 days)
3. **Measure overall performance** – Establish baseline performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1) on the full evaluation set. (1 day)
4. **Disaggregate by subgroup** – Run the same analysis for each demographic group and subgroup. Compute: selection rates, error rates by type (false positive, false negative), and outcome distributions. Use multiple fairness metrics – no single metric captures all relevant forms of unfairness. (1-2 days)
5. **Identify and assess disparities** – Where do group-level metrics diverge significantly? Assess severity: magnitude of disparity, number of affected individuals, nature of harm (false positives vs. false negatives often cause different harms). Statistical significance testing is required. (1 day)
6. **Investigate root causes** – Is disparity caused by training data imbalance? Label bias? Proxy features? Feature engineering choices? Understanding the cause determines the correct mitigation. (1-2 days)
7. **Apply and re-evaluate mitigations** – Apply pre-processing (data rebalancing), in-processing (fairness constraints), or post-processing (threshold adjustment) mitigations as appropriate. Re-evaluate after each change. Document trade-offs – fairness interventions often affect overall accuracy. (1-3 days)
8. **Document and disclose** – For regulated contexts, produce a structured bias audit report. For all contexts, document findings, mitigations, residual disparities, and the decision to deploy despite known limitations. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Before deploying any AI or algorithmic system making consequential decisions about people
- When DPIA or FRIA identifies discrimination as a risk
- After significant model updates or retraining
- As a recurring Optimize-stage practice for live systems

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for participatory engagement (M78) – bias testing reveals statistical disparities; it does not reveal what harms they cause to affected communities
- When you lack sufficient disaggregated data (address the data collection gap first)
- When the system does not make or influence decisions about people

**Output/Deliverable**: Bias assessment report with disaggregated performance metrics by demographic group, identified disparities, root cause analysis, applied mitigations, residual disparity documentation, and deployment decision rationale.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: quantitative, reproducible, standards-based; quality depends on data quality and demographic coverage
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate disaggregated performance breakdowns from evaluation datasets, flag subgroups with statistically significant performance gaps, and produce structured audit reports for human interpretation. Cannot determine which disparities constitute legally or ethically unacceptable harm – that requires human and legal judgment. Cannot compensate for missing demographic data.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M75, M76

## METHOD 81: AI Red Teaming / Adversarial Testing

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Deliberate, structured probing of an AI system by a team of testers using adversarial prompts, edge-case scenarios, and realistic misuse attempts – to expose harmful, unsafe, discriminatory, policy-breaking, or non-compliant outputs before deployment. Distinct from automated evaluation: red teaming is hypothesis-driven and conducted by humans reasoning about harm in context. Recommended by NIST AI RMF. Mandatory under EU AI Act Article 55(1)(a) specifically for providers of general-purpose AI models (GPAI) with systemic risk – not a general requirement for all high-risk AI providers, who have separate obligations under Articles 9 and 15.

**Origin**: Adapted from military adversarial exercises and security penetration testing (1960s-70s). Applied to AI safety by major AI labs from 2020 onward (Microsoft, Google DeepMind, OpenAI, Anthropic). Formalised in NIST AI RMF (2023). Mandated for GPAI systemic risk providers by EU AI Act Article 55(1)(a) (2024).
**Authoritative Source**: NIST. (2023). *AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)*. NIST AI 100-1 (MEASURE function, adversarial testing). Microsoft. (2024). *Azure OpenAI Red Teaming Guide*. learn.microsoft.com. For GPAI mandate: EU AI Act, Article 55(1)(a), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. Perez, E. et al. (2022). "Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models." *arXiv 2202.03286*.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate, Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: diverse testers by background, domain expertise, and lived experience required
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: scope/harm taxonomy + team assembly + open-ended probing + targeted test-case build + systematic testing + mitigation loop
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: diverse testing team time + facilitation + possible external specialist red team

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define scope and harm taxonomy** – What system are you testing (base model, RAG pipeline, fine-tuned model, application layer)? What harm categories are in scope: harmful content generation, jailbreaks, discriminatory outputs, privacy violations, manipulation, misuse for illegal purposes? Build a harm taxonomy before testing begins. (1-2 days)
2. **Assemble a diverse red team** – Include people with different backgrounds, domain expertise, and lived experience of the harms you are testing for. Monoculture red teams systematically miss harm categories outside their experience. For high-stakes systems, include external specialists. (1-2 days preparation)
3. **Open-ended exploration** – Testers freely probe the system using their judgment, trying to elicit harmful outputs. The goal is to generate a list of harms the system exhibits – not to measure them. Document every finding with the prompt, output, and harm category. (2-5 days)
4. **Develop targeted test cases** – From the open-ended findings, create a structured set of adversarial test cases for each harm category. Include edge cases, multi-turn scenarios, and deployment-context-realistic prompts. (1-2 days)
5. **Systematic guided testing** – Run the targeted test cases systematically. Measure pass/fail rates per harm category. This generates the hypothesis list for automated evaluation at scale. (1-2 days)
6. **Document findings** – For each harm: describe the attack vector, representative examples, severity, frequency, and recommended mitigation. Distinguish what can be mitigated from what remains as residual risk. (1 day)
7. **Feed into product changes** – Map findings to mitigations: prompt engineering, fine-tuning, output filtering, UX safeguards, human review triggers. Re-test after mitigation. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- Before deploying any AI system that generates content, takes actions, or influences decisions in ways that could cause harm
- After significant model updates, prompt changes, or system integrations
- When DPIA or FRIA (M83) identifies safety or misuse risk
- As a recurring Optimize-stage practice for live AI products

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for automated evaluation – red teaming generates the harm hypothesis list; automated evaluation measures it at scale
- As a one-time pre-launch exercise treated as permanent sign-off
- With a homogeneous internal team for systems affecting diverse populations

**Output/Deliverable**: Red team report: harm taxonomy with coverage, findings per harm category, severity ratings, representative examples, mitigations applied, and residual risk documentation. For regulated GPAI models, this feeds into Article 55 documentation obligations.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate-Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: systematic human adversarial testing produces findings that automated evaluation misses; coverage depends on team diversity, scenario design quality, testing time, and whether findings are paired with automated evaluation at scale
**AI Augmentation**: AI can generate adversarial prompt variants from a seed scenario, cluster findings by harm category, and draft the harm taxonomy before the session. Cannot conduct red teaming itself – AI-generated adversarial prompts miss the contextually-grounded, socially-aware harm scenarios that human testers with lived experience identify.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M77

## METHOD 82: Post-market Compliance Monitoring and Incident Review

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Ongoing monitoring of a live product for harmful outcomes, rights failures, bias drift, serious incidents, and user complaints signalling compliance failures – with structured review and corrective action when issues are found. Distinct from standard product analytics (M55–M59), which track behavioral and business metrics: this method tracks rights, safety, and compliance signals. EU AI Act Article 72 requires high-risk AI providers to have a post-market monitoring plan; incident reporting and serious incident review obligations apply alongside.

**Origin**: Rooted in post-market surveillance practices from regulated industries (FDA medical device post-market surveillance, pharmaceutical pharmacovigilance). Applied to AI and software products through EU AI Act Article 72 (2024) and NIST AI RMF GOVERN and MANAGE functions (2023). Incident review practices established in information security (NIST SP 800-61) applied to compliance context.
**Authoritative Source**: EU AI Act, Article 72 (post-market monitoring), Articles 73-77 (incident reporting), Regulation (EU) 2024/1689. NIST. (2023). *AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0)*. NIST AI 100-1 (GOVERN and MANAGE functions). NIST. (2012). *Computer Security Incident Handling Guide*. NIST SP 800-61 Rev. 2 (incident review methodology).

**Discovery Stage**: Optimize
**Space**: Solution Space
**Space Notes**: monitoring live product outputs and impacts
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: legal, product, data science, customer support
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: Ongoing
**Time Order**: Ongoing
**Time Notes**: setup + signal monitoring + incident review cadence + corrective-action review
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: internal monitoring/review time + optional compliance monitoring tooling

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define compliance signals to monitor** – What would indicate a compliance failure? Examples: user complaints mentioning privacy, discrimination, or harm; support tickets in specific categories; bias drift in model outputs; legal notices or regulatory enquiries; serious incident reports. Define thresholds that trigger escalation. (1-2 days)
2. **Set up monitoring infrastructure** – Route complaints and support tickets through a compliance-category classification system. Implement bias drift metrics on live model outputs where applicable. Configure alerting for threshold breaches. (3-5 days)
3. **Establish a review cadence** – Monthly or quarterly structured review of all compliance signals. Assign a cross-functional owner for each signal category. (1 day setup)
4. **Run structured incident reviews** – For each flagged incident: describe what happened, assess impact on individuals' rights or safety, identify root cause, determine whether it constitutes a legally reportable serious incident (EU AI Act Article 73 criteria), and document the corrective action taken. (Per incident: 2-4 hours)
5. **Track residual risks from pre-launch assessments** – Review whether risks identified in pre-launch DPIAs, FRIAs, or bias assessments have materialised. Update risk ratings accordingly. (During each review cycle)
6. **Report to the DPO and legal team** – Provide structured summary of compliance monitoring findings at each review. Escalate any potential serious incidents immediately. (Per review cycle)
7. **Close the loop on corrective actions** – Document what changed in the product as a result of monitoring findings. If a risk cannot be mitigated, document the residual risk acceptance decision and its owner. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- As best practice after launching any product handling personal data at scale or making algorithmic decisions – regardless of whether a formal legal obligation applies
- When EU AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring obligations apply (mandatory for high-risk AI system providers)
- When DPIA or FRIA identified residual risks to track after launch
- After any significant model, data, or deployment context change

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for pre-launch compliance work – monitoring finds problems that escaped pre-launch assessment, not structural risks that should have been addressed earlier
- When monitoring outputs are not connected to a corrective-action process (monitoring without action is theater)

**Output/Deliverable**: Post-market monitoring report (monthly/quarterly): compliance signal summary, incidents reviewed, root cause analyses, corrective actions taken, residual risk updates, and regulatory reporting log.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: ongoing operational signals (complaints, incidents, drift metrics) interpreted through expert and legal review; most reliable indicator of actual compliance failures in production but requires judgment to distinguish signal from noise and to determine legal significance
**AI Augmentation**: AI can classify complaints and tickets by compliance signal type, detect anomalies in subgroup performance indicating bias drift, and generate structured incident summaries for human review. Cannot determine whether a pattern constitutes a legally reportable serious incident, cannot substitute for legal judgment, and cannot make corrective-action decisions.

## METHOD 83: Fundamental Rights Impact Assessment (FRIA)

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Assessment of how a high-risk AI system may affect fundamental rights – including non-discrimination, privacy, dignity, due process, and freedom of expression – in its real deployment context. Under EU AI Act Article 27, certain deployers must complete a FRIA prior to first use. Complements the DPIA (M76), which addresses privacy risk specifically: the FRIA addresses the broader fundamental rights picture. Conceptually related to Human Rights Impact Assessment (HRIA) practice used in the broader digital-rights and corporate due-diligence context.

**Origin**: Derived from human rights impact assessment practice (UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights, 2011; Ruggie Framework). Applied to AI specifically through the EU AI Act (2024) as a mandatory pre-deployment requirement for qualifying deployers. Article 27(5) requires the AI Office to develop a standard questionnaire template for conducting FRIAs.
**Authoritative Source**: EU AI Act, Article 27, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (FRIA obligation for qualifying deployers). UN. (2011). *Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights*. United Nations (Ruggie Framework). European Union Agency for Fundamental Rights (FRA). *Getting the future right: Artificial intelligence and fundamental rights*. fra.europa.eu. For HRIA methodology: Danish Institute for Human Rights. *Human Rights Impact Assessment Guidance and Toolbox*. humanrights.dk.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Both
**Space Notes**: assessing deployment context – Problem Space; assessing system design – Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Medium-High
**Complexity**: Moderate-Complex
**Team Size**: Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: legal/compliance, product, data science; domain or civil-society input strengthens output
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: mandatory for qualifying EU AI Act deployers; best practice for any organisation deploying consequential AI
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: scope/obligation check + deployment-context description + affected-group analysis + rights assessment + mitigation + consultation + documentation
**Cost Range**: Medium
**Cost Notes**: internal legal/compliance/product time + possible external rights or domain expert support

**How to Apply**:
1. **Determine scope and obligation** – Does EU AI Act Article 27 apply? Qualifying deployers: (a) public-law bodies and private entities providing public services deploying most Annex III high-risk AI systems; (b) all deployers of Annex III point 5(b) systems (creditworthiness/credit scoring) and 5(c) systems (life and health insurance risk). If in scope, FRIA is mandatory before first use. If not in scope, the method is still valuable best practice. (1-2 hours, with legal)
2. **Describe the system and its deployment context** – What does the system do? Where is it deployed? Who operates it? Who is affected? What decisions does it influence? Be specific about the real use context, not the intended use in isolation. (Half day)
3. **Identify affected people and groups** – Who interacts with the system directly? Who is affected by its outputs indirectly? Which groups may be disproportionately affected? Consider: people subject to automated decisions, marginalised groups, people with disabilities, children, groups with limited means to challenge decisions. (Half day)
4. **Assess fundamental rights impacts** – For each relevant right (non-discrimination, privacy, dignity, effective remedy, due process, freedom of expression, others as applicable), assess: is the right affected? How? With what likelihood and severity? Consider both direct impacts (the system makes a decision) and indirect impacts (the system changes how a human makes a decision). (1-2 days)
5. **Identify mitigations** – For each identified impact: what changes to system design, deployment practice, human oversight, or redress mechanisms would reduce the impact? Document which mitigations are feasible and which residual impacts remain. (1 day)
6. **Consult affected communities** – For significant impacts, incorporate input from affected individuals or their representatives (link to M78). Document the consultation and how it influenced the assessment. (As needed; adds 1-2 weeks if done properly)
7. **Document and make available** – Record the FRIA, store it, and make it available to competent authorities on request. Schedule a review when the system or its deployment context changes materially. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Before deploying any high-risk AI system in the EU (mandatory for qualifying deployers under Article 27)
- To complement a DPIA when the system involves broader rights risks beyond privacy
- When stakeholders require evidence that fundamental rights have been considered before deployment

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for a DPIA when GDPR Article 35 applies – FRIA and DPIA are complementary, not interchangeable; run both when both apply
- Before the deployment context and intended use are sufficiently defined
- As a legal-team-only exercise – the assessment requires knowledge of how the system actually works in practice

**Output/Deliverable**: FRIA report documenting: system and deployment context description, affected people and groups, rights impact assessment per right, identified mitigations, residual impacts, consultation record, and review schedule.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Legal Assessment
**Evidence Notes**: structured, legally-anchored assessment; quality depends on specificity of deployment context analysis and quality of stakeholder input
**AI Augmentation**: AI can draft a structured FRIA template from system documentation and deployment context description, and generate a list of affected rights and affected groups to review and extend. Cannot assess actual rights impact – that requires human judgment, legal expertise, and ideally input from affected communities (M78).

## METHOD 84: Legitimate Interests Assessment (LIA)

**Category**: Compliance
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Specialist
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Structured three-part assessment used when an organisation intends to rely on GDPR Article 6(1)(f) – legitimate interests – as the lawful basis for personal data processing. Tests: (1) Purpose test – is the processing for a genuine, legitimate purpose? (2) Necessity test – is the processing limited to what is necessary? (3) Balancing test – do the controller's interests override the rights and interests of data subjects? If the balancing test fails, legitimate interests cannot be used and another lawful basis must be found or the processing must not proceed. A documented LIA is the standard and strongly recommended way to demonstrate that the Article 6(1)(f) conditions are met – not itself a named statutory instrument, but the recognised operational mechanism. Product teams are increasingly expected to participate in the three-part test, not only receive the outcome.

**Origin**: Derived directly from GDPR Article 6(1)(f) and its predecessor in EU Directive 95/46/EC. The three-part test structure was formalised in EDPB (WP29) guidance and is the ICO's recommended approach. The "legitimate interests" basis has existed in EU data protection law since 1995 but became practically important to product teams following GDPR enforcement (2018 onward).
**Authoritative Source**: GDPR, Article 6(1)(f), Regulation (EU) 2016/679. EDPB (WP29). *Opinion 06/2014 on the Notion of Legitimate Interests of the Data Controller under Article 7 of Directive 95/46/EC*. WP 217. ICO. *Legitimate interests*. ico.org.uk (includes LIA template and worked examples). EDPB has since published additional guidance on legitimate interests under Article 6(1)(f) – check edpb.europa.eu for the latest adopted guidelines.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Space Notes**: decision about lawful basis must be made before processing begins
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: led by legal/privacy with product input
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: Hours to days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: processing/basis definition + purpose test + necessity test + balancing test + documentation + review trigger
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal legal/privacy counsel time + product input

**How to Apply**:
1. **Identify the processing activity and intended basis** – What data are you processing? For what purpose? Why are you considering legitimate interests rather than consent, contract, or legal obligation? Document the reason you believe this basis is appropriate before running the test. (1-2 hours)
2. **Purpose test** – Is the purpose genuine and legitimate? Legal purposes are legitimate. Purposes that serve only the controller's commercial interest at the expense of data subjects are not. Ask: would a reasonable person understand and accept this purpose, even if they might prefer data not be used this way? (1-2 hours)
3. **Necessity test** – Is the processing limited to what is necessary to achieve the stated purpose? Could the purpose be achieved with less data, less intrusive processing, or a less privacy-invasive approach? The test fails if any less-intrusive alternative would achieve the same goal. (1-2 hours)
4. **Balancing test** – Do the controller's interests override the data subjects' rights and interests? Consider: the nature of the data (more sensitive = higher weight to data subject interests), reasonable expectations (would data subjects expect this processing?), potential harm, the power imbalance between controller and data subject. Document the reasoning in both directions. (2-4 hours, with legal)
5. **Document the outcome** – Record the assessment with the reasoning for each part of the test. If the assessment passes, link it to the Article 30 RoPA entry for this processing activity. If it fails, document that legitimate interests cannot be relied upon and identify the correct action (consent, legal obligation, contract, or stop processing). (1-2 hours)
6. **Review when circumstances change** – The LIA is not permanent. If the purpose, the data, or the context changes materially, the assessment must be re-run. Build in a review trigger. (Ongoing)

**When to Use**:
- Before relying on legitimate interests as the GDPR lawful basis for any processing activity
- When a data-flow map (M75) has identified processing activities without a clear lawful basis
- When deciding whether to use consent or legitimate interests for a feature – an LIA makes the decision defensible

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a mechanism to avoid obtaining consent when consent is the appropriate basis – legitimate interests cannot be used for processing requiring explicit consent (special category data, most direct marketing, processing without a compelling legitimate purpose)
- When the conclusion is predetermined – the balancing test must be genuinely conducted; a pro-forma LIA that always passes does not meet GDPR requirements

**Output/Deliverable**: LIA documentation: processing description, purpose test outcome, necessity test outcome, balancing test outcome with reasoning, and overall conclusion on whether legitimate interests can be relied upon.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Legal Assessment
**Evidence Notes**: structured legal assessment; quality depends on honesty and thoroughness of the balancing test reasoning
**AI Augmentation**: AI can draft the three-part assessment structure from a processing description and identify analogous EDPB and ICO guidance for the use case. Cannot determine whether the balancing test is met – that requires qualified privacy counsel.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M75, M76

## METHOD 88: JTBD Framework (Forces of Progress)

**Category**: Strategic Framing
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: The conceptual and analytical framework underlying Jobs-to-be-Done: users "hire" and "fire" products when trying to make progress in a specific context. The Four Forces diagram maps why people switch – Push (frustration with current situation) and Pull (attraction to new solution) drive switching, while Habit and Anxiety resist it. Applied after JTBD Interviews (M2) to synthesise switching forces data into a structured model that explains adoption and churn better than demographics or stated preferences.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – the JTBD Forces of Progress framework reframes user behaviour as a set of forces (push, pull, anxiety, habit) driving or blocking adoption decisions. It is a theoretical lens for designing better interview questions and interpreting data, not a standalone evidence-generation method. Use it to sharpen M1/M2 interview design and analysis.

**Origin**: Conceptual foundation by Clayton Christensen (Harvard Business School) in Competing Against Luck (2016). Four Forces model and Switch Interview methodology developed by Bob Moesta and Chris Spiek (The Re-Wired Group). Distinguished from the interview method (M2) – this is the analytical framework for interpreting the interview data.
**Authoritative Source**: Christensen, C., Hall, T., Dillon, K. & Duncan, D. (2016). *Competing Against Luck: The Story of Innovation and Customer Choice*. Harper Business. Also: Moesta, B. (2020). *Demand-Side Sales 101*. Lioncrest Publishing. Kalbach, J. (2020). *The Jobs To Be Done Playbook*. Rosenfeld Media.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: especially powerful for Scaleup and Enterprise navigating market expansion or fighting churn
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: interview review + force mapping + pattern aggregation + strategic implication synthesis
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal synthesis time + completed JTBD interview input

**How to Apply**:
1. **Gather JTBD interview data** – This framework requires completed M2 (JTBD/Switch) interviews with real switchers and quitters. Do not apply the framework to assumptions. (Pre-work: interviews done)
2. **Map Push forces** – For each interview, identify what was frustrating about the current situation that pushed the user toward change. Quote directly from interview transcripts. What specific pain, friction, or limitation triggered the search for something new? (2-3 hours)
3. **Map Pull forces** – What attracted the user toward the new solution? What did they imagine life would be like after switching? What did the new solution promise? (2-3 hours)
4. **Map Habit forces** – What was comfortable and familiar about the current situation? What would the user have to give up or relearn? What was "good enough" about the status quo? (1-2 hours)
5. **Map Anxiety forces** – What fears or uncertainties did the user have about switching? What might go wrong? What was the risk of the new solution not delivering? (1-2 hours)
6. **Build the forces model** – Aggregate the patterns across all interviews into a single Forces of Progress diagram. Identify which forces are most commonly cited and most emotionally intense – these are the levers for product strategy. (Half day)
7. **Derive strategic implications** – What should the product do to amplify Push (make the current situation feel more frustrating), strengthen Pull (make the promise clearer), reduce Anxiety (reduce perceived risk of switching), and work with Habit (ease the transition)? (Half day with product team)

**When to Use**:
- After M2 (JTBD Interviews) to synthesise switching forces data into a structured model
- When the team is trying to understand why users switch to or away from the product
- When demographic segmentation is not predictive and behavioral/job-based segmentation is needed
- When innovation is stalling because the team is focused on features rather than the progress users are trying to make

**When NOT to Use**:
- For incremental optimization of a well-understood product (analytics and A/B testing are faster)
- When no JTBD interviews have been conducted – the framework requires real switcher data to populate; assumptions produce the wrong jobs
- As a one-time workshop exercise rather than an ongoing analytical lens

**Output/Deliverable**: Four Forces diagram populated with direct evidence from JTBD interviews, with strategic implications document mapping forces to product decisions.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: grounded in real switcher interviews; can be highly informative for adoption strategy when applied rigorously; strength depends on interview quality and sample
**AI Augmentation**: Analyze interview transcripts for language patterns that map to the four forces, suggest force categorizations for researcher validation, and identify which forces appear strongest across a set of switcher interviews. Cannot infer the forces without real JTBD interview data – the framework requires real switcher stories to be meaningful.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M2

## METHOD 89: Capability Contract Workshop

**Category**: Strategic Framing
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Framework
**Short Description**: A structured workshop to define the explicit boundaries of an AI system before committing to build – specifying what it is Allowed to do, Forbidden from doing, what Evidence it must provide, what data it can Retrieve, how it Escalates out-of-scope requests, and a Version identifier. Running the Contract during Explore surfaces feasibility blockers, data permission gaps, and compliance constraints that no amount of user research can reveal. **Author-originated framework** (Amodiovalerio Verde / The Thinking Lens, 2026) – not an established external method; included because it addresses a real gap in AI product discovery practice.
**Context Tool Note**: A context tool – the Capability Contract does not generate user evidence. It generates a scoping specification that reveals where feasibility, data, and compliance risks live. Use it to direct which discovery methods to run next: gaps in Retrieval Scope → Data Readiness Assessment (M91); gaps in Forbidden/Evidence → DPIA (M76) or Privacy Threat Modeling (M77).
**Origin**: Proposed by Amodiovalerio Verde (2026) as Station 1 of the Semantic Supply Chain framework, developed from enterprise AI deployment practice. Formalises a scoping discipline that emerged from observing AI projects fail due to undefined system boundaries – not from academic literature. The framework treats AI capabilities like API contracts: versioned, explicit, enforceable.
**Authoritative Source**: Verde, V. (2026). "The Semantic Supply Chain: A Product Lens on Enterprise GenAI." The Thinking Lens. https://www.the-thinking-lens.com/the-semantic-supply-chain-a-product-lens-on-enterprise-genai/. Also: Verde, V. (2026). SSC Course, Part 1 (Tokens and Boundaries). For the contract-as-specification concept: Richardson, L. & Amundsen, M. (2013). RESTful Web APIs. O'Reilly Media.
**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Solution Space
**Space Notes**: Run before architectural decisions; surfaces constraints on what the solution can be
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Feasibility; secondary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Low
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5), Cross-functional (5+)
**Team Notes**: Requires PM + tech lead + legal or compliance representative. Without legal present, Forbidden and Required Evidence sections are incomplete.
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: Highest value for AI features in regulated industries, high-stakes workflows (finance, healthcare, HR), or any system where the AI takes consequential actions. Less necessary for pure content generation with no system writes.
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Yes
**Time to Execute**: Half-day to 1 day
**Time Order**: Hours
**Time Notes**: team assembly + contract drafting + constraint classification + capability/data audit + documentation + circulation
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal workshop time + shared document/whiteboard
**How to Apply**:
1. **Assemble the team** – PM, tech lead, data/AI engineer, legal or compliance representative, and business owner
2. **Fill in each Contract dimension** – for the specific AI feature:
   - **Allowed Actions**: what the AI can do ("retrieve invoice data for the requesting user", "summarise meeting notes", "recommend next action")
   - **Forbidden Actions**: explicit prohibitions ("cannot approve payments", "cannot access data outside user's role", "cannot generate external communications autonomously")
   - **Required Evidence**: what provenance must accompany outputs ("all recommendations must cite source document and confidence level", "all retrievals must log data source")
   - **Retrieval Scope**: which data sources the AI can access, with permission model ("ERP data, permissioned by user role; no cross-tenant access")
   - **Escalation**: what happens when the AI encounters an out-of-scope request ("surface to human reviewer", "return explicit refusal with reason")
   - **Version**: tag the Contract (v1.0)
3. **Classify each Forbidden item** – technical constraint, legal constraint, or business policy – this surfaces the risk type
4. **Audit Required Evidence** – against technical capability: can the system actually provide that provenance?
5. **Audit Retrieval Scope** – against data availability and permissions – gaps here become Data Readiness Assessment (M91) inputs
6. **Document as a versioned artefact** – distribute to all stakeholders before architecture decisions
**When to Use**:
- At the start of any AI feature development, before architecture decisions are made
- When integrating AI into regulated or high-stakes workflows
- When the team is not aligned on what the AI "is" and "isn't"
- Before security or compliance review – the Contract is the input document
- When evaluating whether to build vs buy vs integrate an AI capability
**When NOT to Use**:
- For pure UI features with no AI judgment, generation, or autonomous action
- When the AI feature is fully standardised with no customisation (e.g., commodity spell-check)
- As a substitute for legal review – the Contract is a discovery tool; formal legal assessment is separate (M76, M84)
**Output/Deliverable**: Versioned Capability Contract document specifying Allowed Actions, Forbidden Actions, Required Evidence, Retrieval Scope, Escalation paths, and Version identifier. Serves as the scoping input for technical architecture and compliance review. Gaps in Retrieval Scope and Forbidden sections become the discovery backlog for M76, M77, M91.
**Evidence Strength**: N/A
**Evidence Type**: Synthesis / Meta
**Evidence Notes**: Produces a structured specification, not evidence; value is in surfacing constraints before they become blockers
**AI Augmentation**: AI can draft an initial Contract from a feature brief – proposing common Allowed/Forbidden patterns for similar AI feature types, flagging retrieval scope conflicts, and identifying gaps in Evidence requirements. The workshop remains essential: the team validates, adjusts, and signs off. AI drafts the v0; humans own the v1.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 90: AI Capability Assessment

**Category**: Experimentation
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A structured probe to determine whether available AI technology – a foundation model, API, fine-tuned model, or internal ML system – can meet the performance threshold required for a specific feature to be viable. Run against a curated sample of real representative tasks before committing to development. Answers the question "can the AI actually do this well enough?" with evidence, not assumption.
**Origin**: Emergent practice in AI product development; no single canonical source. Grounded in ML model evaluation traditions (benchmark testing, held-out test sets) adapted for product discovery decision-making. NIST AI Risk Management Framework (2023) includes structured capability evaluation (TEVV – Test, Evaluate, Verify, and Validate) as a core practice. Note: EU AI Act (2024) conformity assessment for high-risk systems is a separate regulatory obligation with its own requirements – it is not the same as this discovery-stage capability probe, and the Act does not formalise this specific product discovery practice. The product discovery framing – running capability tests before committing to build – is a practitioner synthesis.
**Authoritative Source**: NIST (2023). AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0). National Institute of Standards and Technology. doi:10.6028/NIST.AI.100-1. Also: European Parliament (2024). EU Artificial Intelligence Act. Official Journal of the European Union. For evaluation methodology: Ribeiro, M.T., Wu, T., Guestrin, C. & Singh, S. (2020). "Beyond Accuracy: Behavioral Testing of NLP Models with CheckList." ACL 2020. doi:10.18653/v1/2020.acl-main.442.
**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Space Notes**: Run when the core feasibility assumption is "AI can do X well enough" – before architectural commitment
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Feasibility
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: Requires at least one person with ML/AI evaluation skills to design the test set and interpret results. A PM alone cannot run this rigorously.
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: Highest value when the AI capability is novel, untested for the specific use case, or where performance below threshold makes the feature non-viable. Less necessary for commodity AI tasks (sentiment classification, language detection) where capability is well-established.
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-5 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: capability claim definition + acceptance-criteria setup + representative test-set assembly + run + scoring + decision
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: API/model execution cost + team evaluation time
**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the capability claim precisely** – "The model can classify X into Y categories with >Z% precision on our specific data"
2. **Set acceptance criteria** – define the viability threshold before running the test
3. **Assemble a representative test set** – 30-100 real cases including typical cases, edge cases, and known failure modes; draw from real user data where possible
4. **Run the test** – through the AI system, model, or API under realistic conditions
5. **Score outputs** – against acceptance criteria: precision, recall, hallucination rate, latency, output quality
6. **Document results** – as: capability confirmed / capability gap / capability conditional (viable with constraints)
7. **Make the go/no-go decision** – with explicit evidence
**When to Use**:
- Before committing engineering resources to an AI feature
- When evaluating a new model or API for a specific use case
- When the team is debating "can current AI do this?" without evidence
- When comparing multiple model options for the same task
- After a model update to detect capability regression
**When NOT to Use**:
- When the capability is well-established and pre-benchmarked for your exact use case
- For non-AI features
- As a substitute for production testing – this is discovery-level signal, not production QA
- When the test set cannot be drawn from real representative data (synthetic test sets produce false confidence)
**Output/Deliverable**: Capability assessment report with test set specification, results against acceptance criteria, confidence rating (Confirmed/Conditional/Gap), and a go/no-go recommendation with explicit rationale. If conditional: list of constraints under which the capability is viable.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: structured test of model behavior under representative conditions – "behavioral" here means AI system behavior, not human user behavior; strength depends heavily on test set quality and representativeness; not statistically generalisable to production volume but sufficient for go/no-go decisions in discovery
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help generate diverse test cases from a seed set, identify edge cases the team hasn't considered, and score outputs against a rubric at scale – significantly reducing the manual evaluation burden. Cannot replace human judgment on what "good enough" means for your specific context.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M89

## METHOD 91: Data Readiness Assessment

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A structured discovery activity to evaluate whether the data required for an AI feature – training data, fine-tuning data, retrieval/RAG data, or evaluation data – is available, representative, permissioned, and quality-sufficient before committing to development. Surfaces the most common silent killer of AI features: the data doesn't exist, isn't accessible, or isn't good enough.
**Origin**: Adapted from data management and ML engineering practice for product discovery. Foundational concept: Lawrence, N.D. (2017). "Data Readiness Levels." arXiv:1705.02245 – proposed a structured maturity model for data availability and usability. Data quality assessment grounded in DAMA International (2017). The product discovery framing – using data readiness as an early-stage feasibility gate before committing to build – is a practitioner synthesis for AI product teams.
**Authoritative Source**: Lawrence, N.D. (2017). "Data Readiness Levels." arXiv:1705.02245. Also: DAMA International (2017). DAMA-DMBOK: Data Management Body of Knowledge, 2nd edition. Technics Publications. For AI-specific data quality in product contexts: Sambasivan, N. et al. (2021). "Everyone wants to do the model work, not the data work." CHI 2021. doi:10.1145/3411764.3445518.
**Discovery Stage**: Explore
**Space**: Solution Space
**Space Notes**: Run before any commitment to train, fine-tune, or build a retrieval pipeline; data situation often determines which solution approach is viable
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Feasibility; secondary: Compliance & Ethics
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: Requires a data engineer or data owner alongside the PM. Cannot be run accurately by product alone – data availability and permissions require technical and legal input.
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: Essential practice for any AI feature requiring proprietary data (fine-tuning, RAG, training) – skipping this step is a common source of AI project failure. Less critical for AI features using only public foundation models with no custom data layer. Legal review required when data involves personal data, third-party licensing, consent boundaries, or IP constraints.
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: Conditional
**Time to Execute**: 1-3 days
**Time Order**: Days
**Time Notes**: Interviews with data owners + audit of existing data assets + gap analysis
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: internal time only – no tooling or participant recruitment required
**How to Apply**:
1. **List data requirements** – training data, fine-tuning examples, retrieval corpus, evaluation set
2. **Assess each data source** – across four dimensions:
   - **Availability**: Does the data exist? Where is it stored? Can the team access it?
   - **Permissions**: Is it permissioned for this AI use case? Who owns it? Are there consent or data processing boundaries?
   - **Quality**: Is it clean, labelled, representative of the target use case, and free of critical biases?
   - **Volume**: Is there enough data to achieve the required model performance or retrieval coverage?
3. **Rate each source** – Green (ready), Amber (gaps but fixable), Red (blocker)
4. **Estimate remediation** – for each Red or Amber: effort and timeline
5. **Identify feature-level impact** – does any data gap kill the feature concept or change the required approach?
6. **Document findings** – as a data readiness map; feed blockers into M89 Capability Contract (Retrieval Scope gaps) and M76 DPIA (permission gaps)
**When to Use**:
- Before committing to any AI feature requiring proprietary or curated data
- When evaluating different AI architectures with different data dependencies
- When proposing to fine-tune or train a model on internal data
- When compliance requires documenting data provenance and consent
- When the team assumes "we have the data" without having verified it
**When NOT to Use**:
- For AI features using only public foundation models via API with no custom data layer
- When data availability is already formally documented and verified
- As a substitute for a full data governance audit – this is discovery-level signal, not compliance certification
**Output/Deliverable**: Data readiness map showing each required data source rated Green/Amber/Red across availability, permissions, quality, and volume. Includes a gap register with remediation effort estimates and a recommendation: proceed / proceed with constraints / pivot approach / abandon.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Expert Judgment
**Evidence Notes**: Structured expert assessment of data assets; strength depends on honesty of data owners and depth of access to actual data; identifies blockers but does not quantify model performance
**AI Augmentation**: AI can help generate the assessment framework for a specific feature type, suggest common data quality dimensions for the domain, and draft the gap analysis document from interview notes. Cannot assess actual data quality or permissions – that requires human access to the data systems.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M89

Distinct from user interviews (M1) by population (non-users who left, not current users) and goal (understanding exit decisions and the forces that drove them, not unmet needs for active users). Related to JTBD Switch Interviews (M2) through the "fire" event – but focused on churn cohorts rather than switchers who adopted a new solution.

Churn analysis as a structured discovery practice distinct from exit surveys is an operational synthesis.

Harper Business (Four Forces applied to product abandonment). For SaaS churn context: Skok, D. (various). "SaaS Metrics" series. forentrepreneurs.com. Bliss, J. (2006). *Chief Customer Officer*. Jossey-Bass.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Viability
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: most valuable when churn is measurable and a meaningful cohort exists; lower value at pre-launch or very early Startup stage before a stable user base
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: churn cohort selection + outreach + scheduling + interviews + synthesis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: participant incentives + recruiter/researcher time

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the churn cohort** – Identify who counts as churned for this study: cancelled in the last 30-90 days, downgraded, did not renew. Narrow to a specific window so the experience is still fresh. (1-2 hours)
2. **Segment by churn type** – Distinguish voluntary churn (active cancellation), involuntary churn (payment failure), and downgrade. Each has different root causes. Focus on voluntary churners first – they made a conscious decision. (Half day)
3. **Prepare outreach** – Write a brief, honest outreach message. State the purpose directly: you want to understand what led them to leave, not to win them back. Offer a meaningful incentive. Personal outreach from a PM or founder converts better than automated email. (1-2 hours)
4. **Run 30-45 minute semi-structured interviews** – Open with: "Walk me through the moment you decided to cancel." Do not defend the product. Your job is to understand their experience, not explain yours. Probe: What changed? What were you hoping would be different? What did you try before cancelling? What did you switch to, if anything? (Per participant)
5. **Map against the Four Forces** – Apply the JTBD Forces framework (M88): what Push forces made staying untenable? What was the final trigger? Were there Anxiety forces about the product that were never resolved? (During synthesis)
6. **Identify exit themes** – Cluster reasons across interviews: product gap, price, competitive alternative, lifecycle change, support failure, onboarding failure. Distinguish "we couldn't solve their problem" from "we solved it poorly." (1-2 days)
7. **Feed into retention and roadmap decisions** – Map themes to product decisions: what would have kept them? What is fixable? What is outside scope? (Team decision session)

**When to Use**:
- When churn rate is above acceptable threshold and you don't know the real reasons
- When survey data gives "too expensive" or "missing features" but no actionable signal
- When preparing a retention initiative and need to distinguish real exit reasons from stated ones
- When NPS or CES scores drop and you need to understand what changed

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for proactive retention work – exit interviews are a lagging indicator; use M60 (Continuous Interviewing) to catch signals before churn occurs
- When you have fewer than 10-15 churned customers to contact (too thin for meaningful patterns)
- When the real purpose is win-back, not learning – churned customers can tell when they are being sold to, and it invalidates the data
- Within 48 hours of cancellation when emotional valence is too high for structured reflection

**Output/Deliverable**: Churn interview synthesis: exit reason taxonomy, frequency by theme, representative quotes, and recommended product or process changes. Optional: Four Forces diagram populated with exit data.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: direct qualitative signal from the most honest population in your user base – people who have already left have little reason to soften feedback; recall bias is reduced when interviews are conducted within 30-90 days of churn; combine with behavioral data (last active date, feature usage before churn) to triangulate
**AI Augmentation**: AI can cluster interview transcripts by exit theme, identify language patterns signalling product gap vs. competitive loss vs. lifecycle exit, and generate a structured synthesis document. Cannot distinguish genuine product feedback from rationalisation – human interpretation of emotional cues in transcripts remains important.

## METHOD 93: Pricing and Willingness-to-Pay Testing

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Survey-based methods to determine acceptable price ranges and purchase-intent thresholds before pricing decisions are committed. Covers two primary instruments: Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter (four questions establishing cheap/expensive/too-cheap/too-expensive thresholds that define an acceptable price range) and Gabor-Granger (iterative purchase-intent questions at fixed price points, producing a demand curve). Lighter-weight and faster than Conjoint Analysis (M69) but limited to price in isolation rather than price as a trade-off against features. Distinct from M69 in that it asks about price alone, not about feature bundles at varying prices.

**Origin**: Van Westendorp, P. (1976). "NSS-Price Sensitivity Meter (PSM) – a New Approach to Study Consumer Perception of Price." *Proceedings of the ESOMAR Congress*. Gabor, A. & Granger, C.W.J. (1966). "Price as an Indicator of Quality: Report on an Enquiry." *Economica* 33(129), 43-70. Both instruments developed independently in pricing research and adopted as practical product and market research tools from the 1980s onward.
**Authoritative Source**: Van Westendorp, P. (1976). "NSS-Price Sensitivity Meter." *ESOMAR Congress Proceedings*. Gabor, A. & Granger, C.W.J. (1966). "Price as an Indicator of Quality." *Economica*, 33(129). Simon, H. & Fassnacht, M. (2019). *Price Management: Strategy, Analysis, Decision, Implementation*. Springer. Also: Monroe, K.B. (2003). *Pricing: Making Profitable Decisions*, 3rd ed. McGraw-Hill.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Viability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: instrument selection + survey design + participant recruitment + data collection + threshold analysis
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: survey tooling + participant incentives or panel access

**How to Apply**:
**Van Westendorp (Price Sensitivity Meter)**:
1. **Define the product and audience** – Describe the product clearly before asking about price. Respondents must understand what they are pricing. (1-2 hours)
2. **Ask the four PSM questions** – At what price would this be: (1) so cheap you would question its quality? (2) inexpensive but worth considering? (3) starting to feel expensive but still worth considering? (4) so expensive you would not consider buying? Presented in this order, not as a scale. (Survey instrument)
3. **Recruit 100-400 respondents** – From your target segment via online panels, existing customer lists, or targeted outreach. (3-5 days)
4. **Analyse intersection curves** – Plot cumulative frequency distributions for each question across price points. The Acceptable Price Range (APR) sits between the "Not too cheap" and "Not too expensive" intersections. The Optimal Price Point (OPP) is where "Not expensive" meets "Not cheap." (Half day)
5. **Interpret directionally** – PSM gives a defensible price range, not a precise optimum. Use to screen out unacceptable price points and anchor internal pricing discussions. (Team session)

**Gabor-Granger (purchase intent at fixed price points)**:
1. **Define price points to test** – Select 5-8 price points across the plausible range. (1 hour)
2. **Present sequentially** – Show each respondent price points in descending order (highest first). Ask: "At this price, how likely are you to purchase?" (5-point scale). Stop when the respondent says unlikely. (Per respondent)
3. **Construct the demand curve** – For each price point, calculate the cumulative percentage willing to purchase. Revenue-maximising price = price × % likely to buy, at the peak of that curve. Apply conversion discounts from stated to revealed behaviour. (Half day)

**When to Use**:
- Before committing to a pricing model for a new product or significant feature
- When internal pricing debate lacks external signal and is stalling decisions
- As a faster and cheaper alternative to Conjoint when price-only data is sufficient
- After a pricing change that produced unexpected churn – to understand where the threshold was crossed

**When NOT to Use**:
- When price is inseparable from feature trade-offs – use Conjoint (M69) instead
- When the product has no comparable, making price questions too abstract for reliable responses
- As the sole input to a pricing decision – stated willingness-to-pay consistently overstates revealed willingness-to-pay; validate with behavioral signals (M23, M48)
- For freemium conversion pricing where social and habit factors dominate rational pricing logic

**Output/Deliverable**: Acceptable Price Range and Optimal Price Point from Van Westendorp, or demand curve and revenue-maximising price from Gabor-Granger. Includes interpretation document with recommended pricing range and key caveats.
**Evidence Strength**: Moderate
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: stated willingness-to-pay systematically overstates revealed willingness-to-pay; useful for directional price ranges and eliminating unacceptable price points, not for precise price optimisation; combine with behavioral pricing experiments (M20, M23) for higher-confidence decisions
**AI Augmentation**: AI can design Van Westendorp or Gabor-Granger survey instruments, analyse intersection curves from response data, and generate pricing range summaries. Cannot correct for the stated-vs-revealed willingness-to-pay gap – behavioral validation is required.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M47, M48

## METHOD 94: Search and Query Log Analysis

**Category**: Analytics
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Systematic analysis of what users search for within a product – including queries entered, zero-result queries, query reformulations, and search-to-action conversion rates – to discover unmet information needs, user intent vocabulary, failed retrieval patterns, and the gap between how users think about content and how the system organises it. Zero-result queries are particularly high-signal: they are a direct inventory of things users expected to find but could not. Valuable for search-heavy products, internal knowledge tools, documentation systems, e-commerce catalogs, and AI products with query or chat interfaces.

**Origin**: Established practice in information architecture and search UX since the late 1990s. Formally described as a product discovery methodology in Rosenfeld (2016). Extended to conversational and AI query interfaces through work on query understanding and conversational information seeking (Zamani et al., 2023).
**Authoritative Source**: Rosenfeld, L. (2016). *Search Analytics for Your Site: Conversations with Your Users*. Rosenfeld Media. Also: Morville, P. & Callender, J. (2010). *Search Patterns*. O'Reilly Media. For AI query context: Zamani, H. et al. (2023). "Conversational Information Seeking." *Foundations and Trends in Information Retrieval*, 17(3-4), 244-456.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Optimize
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Value/Desirability; secondary: Usability
**Effort**: Low-Medium
**Complexity**: Simple-Moderate
**Team Size**: Solo, Small (2-5)
**Best For Company**: Any
**Applicability Notes**: highest value for products with internal search, catalog navigation, documentation, knowledge bases, or AI query interfaces; lower value for simple transactional products with no search surface. Query logs may contain personal data (names, account details, sensitive terms) – GDPR/CCPA log retention and data minimisation obligations apply.
**Needs Existing Users**: Yes
**Needs Live Traffic**: Yes
**Needs Legal Review**: Conditional
**Time to Execute**: Days to 1-2 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: log access + query export + zero-result extraction + query clustering + intent taxonomy + gap synthesis
**Cost Range**: Time-Only
**Cost Notes**: analyst time + log access + optional query clustering or NLP tooling

**How to Apply**:
1. **Access and export query logs** – Pull a representative sample: at minimum 2-4 weeks of data, more if volume is low. Include: raw query string, timestamp, session ID (anonymised), result count, and whether the user clicked a result. (Half day)
2. **Extract zero-result queries** – Separate all queries that returned no results. Review every unique zero-result query before anything else – these directly represent what users expected to find and could not. (Half day)
3. **Cluster queries by intent** – Group semantically similar queries together using manual review (low volume) or clustering tools (high volume). Goal: discover the 10-20 dominant intent categories. Record vocabulary – use the words your users actually use, not the words your system uses. (1-2 days)
4. **Analyse reformulation chains** – Find sessions where a user submitted multiple queries. Reformulation patterns reveal where the first query failed: the user is telling you how they expected the system to respond versus how it did. (Half day)
5. **Measure search-to-action conversion** – For each intent cluster, what percentage of sessions resulted in a click, task completion, or scroll to a result? Low conversion on a high-volume intent is a high-priority gap. (Half day)
6. **Build the gap inventory** – Document: (a) zero-result queries with no content coverage, (b) high-volume queries with low click-through, (c) vocabulary mismatches between users and the system. Each gap is a product or content decision. (1 day)
7. **Feed into product and content decisions** – Map gaps to actions: new content, new features, retrieval improvement, taxonomy change, AI response improvement. Prioritise by query volume and business impact. (Team session)

**When to Use**:
- When users are searching and you have logs to analyse
- Before redesigning navigation, taxonomy, or information architecture – query data shows what users expect to find regardless of how content is organised
- When building or improving an AI query interface, chatbot, or RAG system – zero-result and reformulation patterns directly inform retrieval and coverage gaps
- As a complement to user interviews: interviews reveal why users search, logs show what they actually search for

**When NOT to Use**:
- When search volume is too low for meaningful patterns (fewer than a few hundred queries per analysis window)
- As a substitute for understanding user intent – query analysis reveals what users typed, not why; pair with M1 or M7 to understand underlying jobs
- When query logs contain unredacted personal data and have not been assessed for GDPR/CCPA compliance

**Output/Deliverable**: Search analysis report: zero-result query inventory, dominant intent taxonomy with query volumes, reformulation chains, search-to-action conversion by intent, vocabulary gap map, and prioritised gap register with recommended product or content actions.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: direct behavioral signal of user intent and vocabulary at scale; zero-result queries are an unambiguous indicator of unmet retrieval needs; strength depends on log completeness and search volume; does not explain why users search – pair with qualitative methods to understand underlying jobs
**AI Augmentation**: AI can cluster large query logs by semantic intent, identify synonyms and vocabulary mismatches between user queries and system vocabulary, surface zero-result patterns at scale, and draft the gap register from raw log data. Significantly reduces manual analysis time for high-volume search logs. Cannot infer user motivation from query strings alone – qualitative follow-up is required.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 95: Win/Loss Interviews

**Category**: Generative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: Structured retrospective interviews with buyers who chose your product and buyers who chose a competitor or alternative – conducted after a purchase or non-purchase decision has been made. Unlike sales call analysis, which captures observed sales dynamics in real time, win/loss interviews gather reflective causal explanation from decision-makers once the pressure of the sales process is removed. Buyers are more candid after the decision is finalized. The method reveals the actual reasons for buying or not buying – differentiation drivers, competitive perception, pricing sensitivity, trust signals, and deal-breakers – rather than the sales narrative either side presented during the process. A primary viability and commercial discovery method for B2B products and enterprise software.

**Origin**: Established B2B sales improvement practice, systematized as a product and market research methodology by Almquist et al. and applied broadly in SaaS competitive intelligence frameworks. Formally described as a discovery methodology by Chernow (2020) and applied in Pattabhiraman (2018) in the context of product-market fit validation.
**Authoritative Source**: Chernow, E. (2020). *Win/Loss Analysis: How to Capture and Keep the Business You Want*. Independently published. Almquist, E., Senior, J., & Bloch, N. (2016). "The Elements of Value." *Harvard Business Review*. Pattabhiraman, V. (2018). "The Real Reasons You Win and Lose Deals." *First Round Review*.

**Discovery Stage**: Explore, Validate
**Space**: Problem Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Viability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Risk Notes**: Primary risk addressed is commercial viability – whether the product wins in the market for the right reasons. Secondary risk is value/desirability – understanding which aspects of the value proposition actually matter to buyers and which are irrelevant or actively harmful.
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: requires someone with interviewing skills and sufficient credibility with buyers; ideally conducted by product or research, not sales, to reduce bias in responses
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: most valuable when a product is in active sales motion with trackable win/loss outcomes – typically Series B+ B2B or enterprise; less applicable to self-serve consumer products with no defined sale event; requires access to recently-decided buyers and non-buyers
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 3-6 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: participant identification and outreach + 8-15 interviews (wins and losses combined) + synthesis and pattern coding + competitive insight report
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: primarily interviewer time; participant incentives optional for B2B; no platform or tooling cost beyond interview scheduling and recording

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the cohort window** – Select a discrete cohort: deals closed (won or lost) in the last 60-90 days. Avoid going too far back – buyer recall degrades quickly. Aim for 8-15 interviews split across wins and losses, weighted toward losses (losses are higher signal). (Half day)
2. **Identify and recruit participants** – Work with sales or CRM to identify contacts. For wins: recently onboarded customers. For losses: prospects who chose a competitor or chose to do nothing. Outreach should come from a neutral party (product, research) not from the salesperson who ran the deal. A personal email explaining the non-sales intent improves response rates significantly. (1-2 weeks)
3. **Design the interview guide** – Use a structured but conversational guide. Core question areas: (a) what triggered the search for a solution, (b) how they evaluated options, (c) what their top decision criteria were, (d) how your product compared on those criteria, (e) what the final tipping point was. For losses: probe specifically on what the chosen alternative did better. Do not lead or defend. (Half day)
4. **Conduct interviews** – 45-60 minutes per participant. Record with consent. Start with context-setting before getting to evaluation. The most valuable moments are usually when you stop talking and let the buyer explain their internal decision rationale. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Code and pattern-analyse findings** – Tag responses by: win/loss category, primary decision criterion, competitive comparison mentions, pricing sensitivity signals, and trust/risk factors. Look for patterns that diverge between wins and losses – that divergence is your insight. (2-3 days)
6. **Build the competitive insight report** – Summarise: top win drivers, top loss drivers, competitive positioning gaps, pricing perception, and the criteria buyers prioritised that your messaging did or did not address. Flag any systematic mismatches between what sales presents and what buyers actually evaluate on. (1-2 days)

**When to Use**:
- When you need to understand why you are winning or losing deals – not based on sales team perception, but based on buyer explanation
- Before repricing, repositioning, or changing go-to-market strategy – win/loss data should inform strategic pivots
- When competitive differentiation is unclear or contested internally – this method surfaces what buyers actually perceive as differentiation, which often differs from internal assumptions
- When product-market fit signals are ambiguous – a pattern of losses for a consistent reason is direct PMF signal

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for generative problem discovery (M1, M2) – win/loss is post-decision; it tells you how buyers evaluated, not what job they were trying to do before they started evaluating
- When there are no defined sales events – does not apply to self-serve consumer products with no identifiable buy/no-buy moment
- When fewer than 8 interviews are feasible – below that threshold, patterns are unreliable and risk over-indexing on outliers
- Do not conflate with Sales Call Analysis (M8): that method observes live deal dynamics; this method gathers retrospective buyer explanation after the decision is final

**Output/Deliverable**: Win/loss analysis report: key win drivers, key loss drivers, competitive comparison summary, pricing perception, buyer decision criteria ranked by frequency, messaging gaps, and prioritised product or positioning actions.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Self-report
**Evidence Notes**: high-signal for commercial and viability risk when conducted rigorously with a sufficient cohort; attitudinal (self-reported), so buyers may rationalise or reconstruct reasons post-decision – pair with M8 (Sales Call Analysis) and M57 (Feature Audit/Usage Analytics) to triangulate behavioral and observational evidence; loss interviews are higher signal than win interviews because losers have less social incentive to be polite
**AI Augmentation**: AI can assist with interview guide design, transcript analysis, pattern coding across interviews, and generating a structured synthesis report. Particularly useful for surfacing recurring language and competitive mention patterns across a cohort of transcripts. Cannot replace the interviewer's real-time judgment in probing ambiguous responses.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: —

## METHOD 96: Comparative Usability Testing

**Category**: Evaluative
**Status**: Active
**Practitioner Tier**: Extended
**Type**: Method
**Short Description**: A structured usability study in which participants complete a defined set of tasks using two or more products – your product and a direct competitor, alternative solution, or incumbent workflow – in a controlled session. Unlike standard usability testing (M16), which focuses on absolute performance of a single product, comparative testing produces relative performance data: which product is faster, less error-prone, or preferred on defined tasks. Unlike Preference Testing (M54), which measures stated preference, comparative usability testing measures task performance – time-on-task, error rate, task success – as well as perceived ease. It is the cleanest method for generating defensible competitive usability evidence and for understanding where users switching from an incumbent experience friction. Particularly relevant for switching products, redesigns, and category-entry decisions.

**Origin**: Comparative methodology in usability testing formalised by Nielsen (1993) and extended by Lewis (2012) in ISO 9241-11 standardised measurement frameworks. Competitive benchmarking as a distinct discipline described in Sauro & Lewis (2016). Applied specifically to switching products in the SaaS context through Forrester CX benchmarking methodology.
**Authoritative Source**: Nielsen, J. (1993). *Usability Engineering*. Academic Press. Sauro, J. & Lewis, J. R. (2016). *Quantifying the User Experience: Practical Statistics for User Research* (2nd ed.). Morgan Kaufmann. Lewis, J.R. (2012). "Usability: Lessons Learned… and Yet to Be Learned." *International Journal of Human-Computer Interaction*, 28(9), 663-684.

**Discovery Stage**: Validate
**Space**: Solution Space
**Risk Addressed**: primary: Usability; secondary: Value/Desirability
**Risk Notes**: Primary risk is usability – whether your solution is at least as easy to use as what users already have. Secondary risk is desirability – comparative session data often surfaces what users value about the competitor that your product does not yet provide.
**Effort**: Medium
**Complexity**: Moderate
**Team Size**: Small (2-5)
**Team Notes**: requires a skilled facilitator and someone to observe and note; ideally cross-functional (product + design) – sales should not observe to avoid anchoring on sales impressions
**Best For Company**: Scaleup, Enterprise
**Applicability Notes**: most valuable when entering a category with an established incumbent solution, executing a significant redesign with switching risk, or competing for accounts where competitive comparison is part of the buyer evaluation; requires access to the competitor product or incumbent workflow for the comparison condition; lower value for net-new category creation where there is no incumbent to compare against
**Needs Existing Users**: No
**Needs Live Traffic**: No
**Needs Legal Review**: No
**Time to Execute**: 2-4 weeks
**Time Order**: Weeks
**Time Notes**: task design + participant recruiting + 8-12 sessions + scoring and analysis + comparative report
**Cost Range**: Low
**Cost Notes**: facilitator time + participant recruitment and incentives; no platform cost if using standard moderated methods; adds ~30% overhead versus single-product usability testing

**How to Apply**:
1. **Define the comparison hypothesis** – What are you testing? Frame as a specific task-based question: "Can users complete [task] faster and with fewer errors in our product than in [competitor/incumbent]?" Name the comparison condition explicitly before designing tasks. (Half day)
2. **Select tasks** – Choose 3-6 tasks that represent the core use cases where the comparison matters. Tasks must be identical across conditions. Tasks should be realistic and outcome-defined (not "click the button" but "find and apply a discount code to your cart"). (Half day)
3. **Recruit participants** – Target users who are familiar with at least one of the comparison conditions – ideally current users of the incumbent. Aim for 8-12 participants split across conditions using a counterbalanced within-subjects or between-subjects design to control for learning effects. (1-2 weeks)
4. **Conduct sessions** – 60-90 minutes per participant. Counterbalance order to avoid primacy effects. Measure per task: time-on-task, error count, task success (binary or partial), and post-task satisfaction rating (e.g. Single Ease Question, 1-7 scale). After all tasks: comparative preference and preference rationale. (1-2 weeks)
5. **Score and compare** – Calculate per-product, per-task means for: task completion rate, time-on-task (geometric mean), error rate, and SEQ score. Use confidence intervals – with 8-12 participants, differences must be large to be meaningful. Focus on directional patterns, not individual data points. (2 days)
6. **Synthesise competitive findings** – Identify: tasks where your product is clearly faster or more accurate, tasks where competitor wins, and tasks where switching friction is highest (highest error rate + lowest SEQ on transition tasks). Map to design decisions. (1 day)

**When to Use**:
- When entering a market with an established incumbent – this method directly answers "can our product displace what users already use?"
- Before a major redesign of a core workflow – test the proposed redesign against the old version, not just in isolation
- When competitive differentiation claims are contested – "easier to use than [competitor]" is a testable hypothesis
- When sales teams report usability objections during deals – comparative data is concrete counter-evidence or a direct improvement mandate

**When NOT to Use**:
- As a substitute for standard usability testing (M16) – run standard usability testing first to identify baseline issues before adding the comparative complexity
- When no comparable incumbent or competitor exists – comparative testing requires a valid comparison condition; testing against a hypothetical baseline is not this method
- When the product is in early prototype stage – comparative testing is most valuable when your product is close enough to the comparison condition to be a fair contest; do not run a wireframe against a mature product
- Do not conflate with Preference Testing (M54), which measures stated preference; this method measures task performance and may diverge from stated preference

**Output/Deliverable**: Comparative usability report: per-task completion rates, time-on-task comparison, error rates, SEQ scores by product and task, participant-level observations, qualitative themes from think-aloud, and design priority recommendations based on competitive gaps.
**Evidence Strength**: Strong
**Evidence Type**: Behavioral
**Evidence Notes**: strongest usability evidence when tasks are well-defined and participants are representative; comparative metrics are directionally reliable even with moderate sample sizes; does not predict whether users will switch – switching decisions involve factors beyond usability (cost, inertia, integration, procurement); pair with M48 (Pre-sales/LOI) and M67 (Sales-Led Prototype Testing) for commercial validation of switching intent
**AI Augmentation**: AI can assist with task design, session note analysis, pattern synthesis across sessions, and generation of the comparative scoring table and report. Transcript analysis works well for identifying recurring friction points and competitor-specific language. Cannot replace the facilitator's real-time judgment and cannot observe behavioral signals from audio-only transcripts.
**Superseded By**: —
**Prerequisites**: M16
